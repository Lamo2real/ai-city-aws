## 6. Survey and interview research: stable craft, unstable samples

### 6.1 Questionnaire design

[DURABLE] Write survey questions about one concept at a time in short, simple language; avoid biased wording; watch for social-desirability and yes-saying; put general questions before specific ones on a topic; and make response options mutually exclusive, exhaustive and logically ordered, with "don't know" or neutral options where appropriate ([AAPOR Best Practices](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/best-practices/)).

[DURABLE] To measure change, do not change the measure. Hold wording, framing and mode constant, and if a change is unavoidable run a split-ballot experiment to quantify its effect ([AAPOR Best Practices](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/best-practices/)). Random assignment to split forms is the way to attribute a difference to wording itself ([Pew, Writing survey questions](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/)).

[DURABLE] Wording moves results by tens of points. In a Pew example from January 2003, support for military action in Iraq fell from 68% to 43% when a clause about casualties was added ([Pew, Writing survey questions](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/)).

[DURABLE] Closed and open questions give different answers. In a 2008 Pew poll, 58% picked "the economy" when it was listed against 35% who volunteered it unprompted, and only 8% gave an unlisted answer in the closed form against 43% in the open form ([Pew, Writing survey questions](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/)).

[DURABLE] Keep opinion response lists to about four or five options, while factual and demographic items can carry more; a 2019 Pew study found forced-choice items more accurate than select-all-that-apply, especially on sensitive topics ([Pew, Writing survey questions](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/)).

[DURABLE] Control response-order effects. Phone respondents favour later options and self-administered respondents favour earlier ones, so randomise unordered lists; never randomise an ordinal scale, but reverse it for a random half of respondents ([Pew, Writing survey questions](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/)).

[DURABLE] Control question-order effects. Pew documents a contrast effect (45% versus 37% support for civil unions in 2003, depending on whether a marriage question came first) and an assimilation effect (81% versus 66% on a 2008 bipartisanship item), and notes that closed questions placed before an open one seed its answers ([Pew, Writing survey questions](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/)).

[DURABLE] Replace agree/disagree items with a forced choice between two statements, because agree/disagree formats attract more agreement from less-educated and less-informed respondents ([Pew, Writing survey questions](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/)).

[DURABLE] Expect social-desirability bias: respondents understate drug and alcohol use and tax evasion and overstate voting, giving and attendance, more so with an interviewer than in web or paper modes; mitigate with face-saving wording ([Pew, Writing survey questions](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/)).

[DURABLE] Pretest every questionnaire with cognitive interviews among people like the target sample, then pilot every procedure from recruitment to data cleaning, and use experiments to choose between wordings ([AAPOR Best Practices](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/best-practices/)).

[DURABLE] Order the questionnaire for flow: group by topic, open with engaging items, do not stack difficult questions, and put demographics late unless they are needed for screening ([Pew, Writing survey questions](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/)). Disclose sensitive content in advance, allow skipping, start with easy questions and keep it short ([AAPOR Best Practices](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/best-practices/)).

[INFERENCE] Scale advice that the researchers could not source: use 5- or 7-point fully labelled scales for attitudes and keep direction consistent.

### 6.2 Sampling, sample size and margin of error

[DURABLE] Probability samples remain the traditional standard; non-probability samples such as opt-in panels need special statistical techniques and careful transparency, and the sampling frame should match the target population ([AAPOR Best Practices](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/best-practices/)).

[DURABLE] Report the margin of sampling error for probability surveys and a credibility interval for non-probability surveys, and be transparent about weighting ([AAPOR Best Practices](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/best-practices/)).

[DURABLE] Benchmarks for margin of error at 95% confidence: a sample of 1,067 gives about plus or minus 3 points and a subgroup of about 160 gives about plus or minus 8 ([Pew, 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2016/09/08/understanding-the-margin-of-error-in-election-polls/)).

[DURABLE] The margin on the gap between two shares in the same sample is roughly double the single-share margin, so plus or minus 3 on each share becomes about plus or minus 6 on the lead, and plus or minus 8 becomes about plus or minus 16 in the subgroup ([Pew, 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2016/09/08/understanding-the-margin-of-error-in-election-polls/)).

[DURABLE] Weighting corrects non-response bias but increases variance through the design effect and shrinks the effective sample size, and the reported margin should include it. Margin of error covers sampling error only and not coverage error, mode effects, misreporting or question wording ([Pew, 2016](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2016/09/08/understanding-the-margin-of-error-in-election-polls/)).

[DURABLE] Size samples for the difference you need to detect. The NIST handbook gives, for a two-sided test of a change in a proportion, N at least [(z for 1 minus alpha over 2, times the root of p0(1 − p0), plus z for 1 minus beta, times the root of p1(1 − p1)), divided by delta] squared, where delta is the absolute difference between p1 and p0, with a continuity correction adding 1/delta. Its worked example, detecting a move from 10% to 20% with one-sided alpha of 0.05 and 90% power, needs about 102 observations, or 112 with the correction ([NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section2/prc242.htm)).

[DURABLE] Reduce non-response with incentives, multiple contacts at different times and by different methods, and varied messages ([AAPOR Best Practices](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/best-practices/)).

[INFERENCE] Standard margin-of-error formula, not fetched in the research but numerically consistent with Pew's 1,067 figure: at 95% confidence the margin for a proportion is 1.96 times the square root of p(1 − p)/n, worst case at p = 0.5, which gives required samples of about 96 for plus or minus 10 points, 384 for 5, 600 for 4, 1,067 for 3 and 2,401 for 2. Multiply by the design effect when weighting, and for small populations apply the finite population correction, adjusted n = n / (1 + (n − 1)/N).

[INFERENCE] B2B SaaS reality: the population of target accounts is often a few hundred to a few thousand, response rates are low and respondents are not a random sample, so margin-of-error arithmetic overstates precision. Report counts alongside percentages and label findings from fewer than about 30 respondents per cell as directional; the cut-off is the researchers' rule of thumb.

### 6.3 Fraud and AI-generated responses, 2025 to 2026

[FAST-MOVING, April 2026] Treat online sample integrity as the main data-quality risk. NORC's April 2026 literature review reports industry estimates of fraud at 15 to 30% of market-research responses and up to 45% on some platforms, one case where usable responses fell from about 75% to 10%, and openly recruited, incentivised surveys with 94 to 95% or more fraudulent or bot responses ([NORC, April 2026](https://www.norc.org/content/dam/norc-org/pdf2026/cpss-research-brief-fraud-lit-review.pdf)).

[FAST-MOVING, 2025] Standard quality checks no longer stop AI respondents. NORC reports that Westwood (2025, PNAS) built an LLM-based synthetic respondent that passed 99.8% of standard data-quality checks ([NORC, April 2026](https://www.norc.org/content/dam/norc-org/pdf2026/cpss-research-brief-fraud-lit-review.pdf)). The figure is cited through NORC; the original paper could not be opened.

[FAST-MOVING, 2025] Real participants also use AI. In one 2025 study reported by NORC, 34% of active online survey participants said they used LLMs to help answer open-ended questions; LLM answers are more homogeneous and can suppress real variation, and response-time signals are less reliable when AI assists ([NORC, April 2026](https://www.norc.org/content/dam/norc-org/pdf2026/cpss-research-brief-fraud-lit-review.pdf)).

[FAST-MOVING, April 2026] Fraud types named by NORC are identity falsification and repeat entries, bots that mimic human timing and evade honeypots, CAPTCHA and IP checks, use of VPNs and CAPTCHA-solving services, and hybrid attacks where a human starts and a bot finishes. Question-specific transformer classifiers reached up to 100% accuracy on the question they were tuned for but transferred poorly across questions (Claassen et al., 2026, via [NORC, April 2026](https://www.norc.org/content/dam/norc-org/pdf2026/cpss-research-brief-fraud-lit-review.pdf)).

[FAST-MOVING, April 2026] NORC's recommended defence is layered, multi-stage checking: reviews tailored to the instrument and population because generic AI detectors and static rules often fail; care not to over-screen legitimate respondents with low digital literacy; collected paradata; pre-specified and disclosed exclusion rules; targeted human review; "reverse shibboleth" questions that the real target population should get wrong; the CATCH framework (configure, assess, triage, corroborate, hone; Stemmer et al., 2026); and probability-based panels with verified contact and PIN entry, which lower but do not remove risk ([NORC, April 2026](https://www.norc.org/content/dam/norc-org/pdf2026/cpss-research-brief-fraud-lit-review.pdf)).

[CONTESTED] [VENDOR] [FAST-MOVING, April 2026] A PsyArXiv preprint from early April 2026, with its lead author at Prolific and funded by Prolific and not peer-reviewed, found fewer than 1% of about 4,800 responses from 12 sample providers flagged as likely non-human text, against about 16% of roughly 400 from a thirteenth provider, Amazon Mechanical Turk. Detection used Prolific's proprietary checker, which outside researchers cannot use; Westwood said the claims cannot be verified externally, and another researcher argued that human fraud remains the larger problem ([Retraction Watch, 30 Apr 2026](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/04/30/are-ai-chatbots-infiltrating-online-survey-data-not-yet-says-new-study/)).

[INFERENCE] The under-1% figure and the 15 to 30% figure are not directly comparable: they cover different populations (vetted panellists versus the whole market), use different definitions (AI-written text versus any fraud), and the lower figure comes from an interested party using an unverifiable tool. Agents should cite both and not pick one.

[FAST-MOVING, 30 Apr 2026] A VU Amsterdam expert panel reported on 30 April 2026 that CAPTCHA is becoming obsolete as a bot filter; that bot networks farming paid panels and ordinary participants pasting open-ends into chatbots are two distinct contaminations needing different countermeasures; that AI text tends to inflate effect sizes, over-confirm hypotheses and misrepresent minorities; that embedded "poison pill" instructions, keystroke and response-time metadata and copy-paste blocking are partial and temporary; and that panel providers should be responsible for verifying respondents ([VU Amsterdam, 30 Apr 2026](https://vu.nl/en/news/2026/ai-and-the-integrity-of-survey-responses)). This is expert opinion.

[DURABLE] Data cleaning is part of the analysis. NORC reports a 2005 study in which removing an 11% share of fraudulent responses changed which results were statistically significant, and notes that LLM-generated answers reduce natural variance ([NORC, April 2026](https://www.norc.org/content/dam/norc-org/pdf2026/cpss-research-brief-fraud-lit-review.pdf)).

[INFERENCE] Minimum 2026 data-quality protocol for any online survey the department commissions, derived from NORC's recommendations: pre-register exclusion rules; prefer the company's own customer lists or verified or probability panels over open links, and never recruit incentivised surveys through open social posts; run layered checks for device and IP duplication, speeding, straight-lining, consistency pairs, at least one open-end reviewed for templated or LLM-style text, and a reverse-shibboleth item; have a human review flagged cases; and report the number screened, the number removed and why.

[GAP] No reliable source was found on the validity of synthetic respondents or LLM-simulated panels as a substitute for human samples, beyond the ICC/ESOMAR disclosure duty. Until one exists, synthetic respondents must not be reported as survey evidence.

### 6.4 Disclosure and respondent duties

[DURABLE] Every survey report discloses sample size, margin of error or credibility interval, weighting, full question text and response options, mode, target population, and sample construction and recruitment, following the AAPOR Transparency Initiative list as summarised on the best-practices page ([AAPOR Best Practices](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/best-practices/)).

[FAST-MOVING, 2025] ICC/ESOMAR Article 4 on primary data collection requires researchers to identify themselves, to disclose at the start any synthetic persona used in an interaction, to keep participation voluntary, to limit re-contact, to give a retention statement, and to honour the right to withdraw and have data deleted ([ESOMAR, ICC/ESOMAR Code](https://esomar.org/representation/icc-esomar-code-of-conduct)).

[FAST-MOVING, 2025] ICC/ESOMAR Article 1 (duty of care) gives participants the right to decline, requires that participation cause no harm, and requires research to be kept clearly separate from non-research activity such as selling, with separate consent. Article 2 requires parental or guardian consent for children and special care for vulnerable people, and Article 3 limits collection to personal data relevant to the research purpose ([ESOMAR, ICC/ESOMAR Code](https://esomar.org/representation/icc-esomar-code-of-conduct)).

[INFERENCE] An AI interviewer or AI-moderated chat must say it is an AI at the start of every interview, on the basis of ICC/ESOMAR Article 4 on synthetic personas. The researchers also point to the EU AI Act Article 50 transparency duty, but its text was not read in this research, so treat that legal basis as unverified and confirm it with Legal.

[INFERENCE] The ICC/ESOMAR Code binds ESOMAR members and those who sign up to it; for a SaaS company's internal research function it is a voluntary benchmark unless an agency it hires is a member. The membership effect was not checked on the page.

### 6.5 Interviews, focus groups and qualitative sample size

[DURABLE] Size interview studies by saturation. At a new-information threshold of 5% or less, saturation was typically reached at 6 to 7 interviews in homogeneous samples and up to 11 to 12 in a more varied one, at which point roughly 78 to 82% (homogeneous) or 62 to 71% (varied) of all themes in the full dataset had been found ([PLOS ONE, Guest, Namey and Chen 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0232076)). Report saturation as base size, run length and threshold, for example "5% or less new information at 6+2".

[DURABLE] For expert and stakeholder input, the Futures Toolkit offers two structured techniques: "Seven Questions", a structured stakeholder interview, and Delphi, which gathers expert opinion over anonymous rounds ([Futures Toolkit HTML](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/futures-toolkit-for-policy-makers-and-analysts/the-futures-toolkit-html)).

[INFERENCE] [GAP] Interview and focus-group method has no retrieved source. As unsourced defaults: use a semi-structured guide of 6 to 10 open questions, ask about past concrete behaviour and not hypotheticals, avoid leading questions, record and transcribe with consent, and debrief immediately. Focus groups, commonly described as 6 to 8 people for 60 to 90 minutes, suit idea generation and language discovery but are prone to dominance and conformity effects and should not be used to estimate incidence. A lead to open next time is https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-interviews/.

### 6.6 Pricing research and choice methods

[DURABLE] Van Westendorp's Price Sensitivity Meter asks four questions: at what price the product is too expensive to consider, so cheap that quality is doubted, starting to seem expensive but still worth considering, and a bargain. The cumulative curves intersect at the point of marginal cheapness (too cheap crossing expensive, the lower bound), the point of marginal expensiveness (too expensive crossing cheap, the upper bound), the indifference price point (expensive crossing cheap) and the optimal price point (too cheap crossing too expensive); the acceptable range runs between the two marginal points ([Wikipedia, Van Westendorp's Price Sensitivity Meter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Westendorp%27s_Price_Sensitivity_Meter)). Read on a tertiary source.

[DURABLE] Van Westendorp measures price perception and not demand; interpretations of the intersections vary and are advocates' claims, not established results; and the method assumes respondents can imagine the price landscape. The Newton-Miller-Smith extension adds purchase-likelihood questions on a 1 to 5 scale at the "expensive" and "cheap" prices to derive a revenue curve ([Wikipedia, Van Westendorp's Price Sensitivity Meter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Westendorp%27s_Price_Sensitivity_Meter)).

[INFERENCE] [GAP] Method selector, unsourced: MaxDiff (best-worst scaling) to rank many features or messages; choice-based conjoint to estimate trade-offs and willingness to pay across feature-and-price bundles; Gabor-Granger for a simple demand curve on a defined product; Van Westendorp for an early acceptable-price range on a new concept; and monadic or sequential-monadic concept tests to compare ideas against a benchmark. All stated-preference methods should be checked against behavioural data such as trials, price tests and win/loss. Conjoint and MaxDiff sample-size rules (Sawtooth), Gabor-Granger and concept testing were not opened; do not quote rules of thumb for them from memory.
