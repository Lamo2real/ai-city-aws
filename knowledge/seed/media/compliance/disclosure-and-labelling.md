**When to use this:** Before publishing anything with an AI-generated or AI-modified element: EU AI Act Article 50, deepfakes, the EU icons, C2PA and SynthID, and YouTube, TikTok, Meta and LinkedIn AI labels. Orientation for a human to confirm.

**Source section:** 17. Disclosure and labelling

### Article 50 in one paragraph

**[FAST-MOVING, since 2 Aug 2026]** Article 50 of the EU AI Act has applied since 2 August 2026, which the European Commission's own pages confirm (checked on a primary source; [European Commission, Code of Practice page, updated 19 Oct 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/code-practice-ai-generated-content); [European Commission, guidelines page, updated 6 Aug 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/guidelines-transparency-ai-generated-content)). A company that publishes realistic AI-generated or AI-manipulated image, audio or video that could be taken as authentic must disclose that clearly at first exposure, with a lighter duty for evidently artistic, creative, satirical or fictional work. The wording of Article 50 in this document comes from a reference site's consolidated text and law-firm notes and not from EUR-Lex (secondary source only; [Article 50, artificialintelligenceact.eu reference text](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)). This is orientation and not legal advice; verify at [EUR-Lex, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, unread](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) or [AI Act Service Desk, Article 50, unread](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-50).

### The deployer's duty for deepfakes

**[FAST-MOVING, since 2 Aug 2026]** Secondary source only: Article 50(4) requires deployers to disclose that image, audio or video content constituting a deepfake has been artificially generated or manipulated. Where the content is part of an evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or analogous work, the duty is limited to disclosing the existence of such content in a way that does not hamper display or enjoyment of the work ([Article 50, artificialintelligenceact.eu reference text](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)). Checked on a primary source: the Commission's icon page summarises a deepfake as AI-generated or manipulated image, audio or video resembling real people, objects, places, entities or events that could falsely appear authentic ([European Commission, EU icons page, updated 13 Oct 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/eu-icons-labelling-ai-generated-content)).

### The text rule and why ordinary marketing text is generally outside it

**[FAST-MOVING, since 2 Aug 2026]** Secondary source only: Article 50(4) also requires deployers to disclose AI-generated or manipulated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest, unless it has undergone human review or editorial control and a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility ([Article 50, artificialintelligenceact.eu reference text](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)). A Swedish law firm reads the text rule as not covering ordinary marketing, and notes that the AI literacy duty in Article 4 has applied to deployers since 2 February 2025 ([CMS Sweden, 28 May 2026](https://cms.law/en/swe/legal-updates/ai-in-advertising-key-aspects-for-advertisers-in-sweden)). The guidelines, as summarised by a law firm, set the human-review bar as a named person with relevant expertise who can approve, change or reject, and say a skim read is not enough ([Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/viewpoints/102nfqm/you-talkin-to-me-operationalising-the-eu-ai-acts-transparency-obligations)).

### Provider marking and the form of disclosure

**[FAST-MOVING, since 2 Aug 2026]** Secondary source only: Article 50(2) requires providers of generative systems to mark outputs in a machine-readable, detectable way, with exemptions for systems that perform an assistive function for standard editing or do not substantially alter the input or its semantics. Article 50(5) requires information to be clear, distinguishable, given at the latest at first interaction or exposure, and accessible ([Article 50, artificialintelligenceact.eu reference text](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)).

### Timing changes from the Digital Omnibus

**[FAST-MOVING, 27 Jul 2026]** Secondary source only: law firms report that the AI Omnibus, cited as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, entered into force on 27 July 2026. It gives providers of generative systems already on the market before 2 August 2026 until 2 December 2026 to meet the Article 50(2) marking duty. Article 50(1), (3) and (4) were not delayed. Content published before 2 August 2026 need not be labelled retroactively ([Goodwin, Aug 2026](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/alerts-technology-dpc-eu-ai-act-transparency-obligations-now-in-force); [Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/viewpoints/102nfqm/you-talkin-to-me-operationalising-the-eu-ai-acts-transparency-obligations); [Paul Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/eu-finalises-transparency-rules-for-ai-generated-content)). The Omnibus also bans AI nudification apps and systems generating child sexual abuse material from 2 December 2026, with fines for the nudification ban reported at up to EUR 35 million or 7% of turnover ([Goodwin, Aug 2026](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/alerts-technology-dpc-eu-ai-act-transparency-obligations-now-in-force)).

### Penalties

**[FAST-MOVING, Aug 2026]** Secondary source only: breaches of Article 50 carry penalties of up to EUR 15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, enforced by national market surveillance authorities ([Goodwin, Aug 2026](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/alerts-technology-dpc-eu-ai-act-transparency-obligations-now-in-force)). **[GAP]** Which Swedish authority supervises Article 50, and any Swedish supplementary law, was not found.

### The Code of Practice and the guidelines

**[FAST-MOVING, 2026]** Checked on a primary source: the Commission's Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content went through a first draft on 17 December 2025 and a second on 3 March 2026, and the final Code was published on 10 June 2026. It had about 190 signatories by the end of July 2026. The Commission and the AI Board confirmed it as an adequate voluntary tool, and non-signatories must show that their own measures are adequate ([European Commission, Code of Practice page, updated 19 Oct 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/code-practice-ai-generated-content)). Secondary source only: the final guidelines on Article 50 were published on 20 July 2026 and are non-binding ([Goodwin, Aug 2026](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/alerts-technology-dpc-eu-ai-act-transparency-obligations-now-in-force); [Paul Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/eu-finalises-transparency-rules-for-ai-generated-content)).

**[FAST-MOVING, 2026]** Secondary source only: the deployer section of the Code, as summarised by a law firm, says labels must be noticeable without user interaction, visible long enough, embedded in the content where possible and perceivable at first exposure; that audible disclaimers are used where no visual is possible; that deployers need proportionate internal processes, staff awareness and feedback routes; and that reliance on the editorial exemption needs documented workflows ([Paul Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/eu-finalises-transparency-rules-for-ai-generated-content)).

**[FAST-MOVING, 2026]** Secondary source only: the provider section, as summarised, calls for at least two machine-readable marking techniques such as signed metadata plus an imperceptible watermark, for users to be barred from stripping metadata, for a free detection mechanism, for watermark-detection interoperability by 2 February 2027, and exempts text under about 150 words from watermarking ([Paul Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/eu-finalises-transparency-rules-for-ai-generated-content); [Jones Day, June 2026](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/06/european-commission-publishes-final-code-of-practice-on-marking-and-labelling-aigenerated-content)).

### The EU icons

**[FAST-MOVING, 13 Oct 2026]** Checked on a primary source: the Commission provides three free icons in SVG and PNG, with no attribution needed and optional use: a basic icon meaning AI was involved, to be paired with a text label or an interactive second layer; a "fully AI-generated" icon; and a "partially AI-modified" icon. There are four colour variants, and user testing found that the basic icon works better with a text label ([European Commission, EU icons page, updated 13 Oct 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/eu-icons-labelling-ai-generated-content)).

**[FAST-MOVING, 13 Oct 2026]** Checked on a primary source: the Commission's placement summary says the icon must be perceivable at first view, not obstructed, embedded in the content unless an equivalent such as an interface overlay is used (creative works are exempt from direct embedding), and still visible when the content is reshared or downloaded. Accessibility advice includes alt text and plain wording. Using the icon does not by itself prove compliance. The Commission's examples of "partially AI-modified" include a real photo of an empty flat furnished with AI ([European Commission, EU icons page, updated 13 Oct 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/eu-icons-labelling-ai-generated-content)).

### A labelling decision sequence for agents

**[INFERENCE]** The researchers propose five questions in order. First, does the asset contain AI-generated or AI-manipulated image, audio or video? If not, stop, but check platform rules for edits. Second, could a reasonable viewer take it as a real person, place, object or event? If so, treat it as a deepfake: a visible label at first exposure, embedded in the asset, plus the platform toggle, using "fully AI-generated" or "partially AI-modified" wording or icon. Third, is it evidently fictional, artistic or stylised, such as a cartoon or obvious fantasy? A discreet disclosure such as a credit line or caption is likely enough under the artistic limb, and platform labels are still set where the platform asks. Fourth, is it only standard editing such as colour, crop, noise removal or upscaling? No Article 50 label is needed; keep the metadata. Fifth, for voice: a cloned or synthetic voice that sounds like a real identifiable person needs consent plus disclosure, while a generic synthetic narrator is lower risk but should be disclosed in audio-only formats when realistic.

**[INFERENCE]** Realistic AI "photography" in ads, such as synthetic models, synthetic product-in-use scenes and AI-furnished rooms, falls within the Commission's own deepfake examples, so marketing teams should assume a label is required. This is an inference from the icon-page examples; the guidelines' text on advertising was not read.

### Provenance: C2PA Content Credentials and SynthID

**[PLATFORM CLAIM] [FAST-MOVING, read Nov 2026]** C2PA publishes the open Content Credentials standard; its site links specification version 2.3, and steering committee members include Adobe, Amazon, the BBC, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Publicis Groupe, Sony, TikTok and Truepic ([C2PA](https://c2pa.org/)). Google DeepMind describes SynthID as an invisible watermark for images, video, audio and text from Google models, designed to survive cropping, filters and compression; anyone can check media through the SynthID Detector or by asking in Gemini, and the detector also covers content from named partners ([Google DeepMind, SynthID](https://deepmind.google/models/synthid/)). Adobe applies Content Credentials automatically to certain Firefly exports ([Adobe Firefly FAQ, updated 10 Nov 2026](https://helpx.adobe.com/firefly/get-set-up/learn-the-basics/adobe-firefly-faq.html)).

**[INFERENCE]** Keep provenance metadata intact through editing and export. Stripping it conflicts with the direction of the Code, removes the automatic platform labels that support compliance, and breaches some vendors' terms (section 11).

### YouTube's AI disclosure rules

**[PLATFORM CLAIM] [FAST-MOVING, read Nov 2026]** YouTube requires creators to disclose realistic altered or synthetic content: a real person appearing to say or do something they did not, altered footage of real events or places, realistic scenes that did not occur, and AI music as the main focus of a video. No disclosure is needed for clearly unrealistic content, beauty filters, upscaling, captions, AI-assisted scripts or thumbnails, or cloning your own voice for voiceover or dubs. The setting is under "AI use" in Studio. YouTube may label automatically from C2PA metadata or its own tools, and those labels cannot be removed ([YouTube Help, disclosing altered or synthetic content, read Nov 2026](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491)).

### TikTok's AI disclosure rules

**[PLATFORM CLAIM] [FAST-MOVING, read Nov 2026]** TikTok requires labels on all AI-generated content with realistic images, audio or video. It prohibits AI likeness of anyone under 18, of adult private figures without permission, of public figures shown endorsing or being endorsed, and fake authoritative sources or crisis events. It labels automatically content carrying C2PA Content Credentials ([TikTok Support, AI-generated content, read Nov 2026](https://support.tiktok.com/en/using-tiktok/creating-videos/ai-generated-content)).

### Meta's AI disclosure rules

**[PLATFORM CLAIM] [FAST-MOVING, 23 Oct 2025]** Meta applies an "AI info" label, renamed from "Made with AI" on 1 July 2024, from industry-standard signals or self-disclosure. Since 12 September 2024, content only edited with AI has the label moved into the post menu while fully generated content keeps a visible label. Advertisers must disclose digitally created or altered political and social-issue ads ([Meta Newsroom, labelling AI content, post last modified 23 Oct 2025](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/04/metas-approach-to-labeling-ai-generated-content-and-manipulated-media/)). **[GAP]** Meta's current advertiser-side AI labelling rules beyond this, and TikTok's ad rules, were not verified.

### Self-regulation: disclosure does not cure a misleading claim

**[DURABLE]** Secondary source only: under the ICC Code marketers remain responsible for AI-produced results. The ICC's responsible-AI guide says AI use alone does not require disclosure, that disclosure may be needed to prevent deception, and that disclosure does not cure a misleading claim. The Swedish advertising ombudsman, Reklamombudsmannen, applies the ICC Code, and Swedish courts refer to it ([CMS Sweden, 28 May 2026](https://cms.law/en/swe/legal-updates/ai-in-advertising-key-aspects-for-advertisers-in-sweden)). **[INFERENCE]** A label never fixes a misleading claim, so product-accuracy QC stays mandatory.

### Two separate labels can apply to one asset

**[INFERENCE]** Branded content, creator collaborations and AI-avatar posts must be marked as advertising from the first view and show the company as sender, independently of any AI label. Two separate labels can therefore be needed on one asset: the advertising marker under marketing law (section 22) and the AI disclosure under the AI Act and platform rules.
