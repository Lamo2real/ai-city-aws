**When to use this:** When setting goals, meetings or decision rights: OKRs, EOS Level 10, 4DX, the minimum viable operating rhythm, RAPID, DACI, RACI and the decision record template.

**Source section:** 5. Operating rhythm and decision rights

### The goal systems converge on the same mechanics

**[INFERENCE]** OKRs, EOS, and 4DX converge on the same mechanics: a few quarterly priorities with owners, a weekly numeric scorecard, a fixed-agenda weekly meeting that spends most of its time solving issues, and explicit single-person decision ownership. No controlled evidence comparing goal systems was found; the framework pages are the owners' own descriptions.

### OKRs

**[DURABLE]** In John Doerr's description, one Objective carries three to five Key Results; the typical cycle is a quarter with Key Results graded at period end, and Objectives may roll over for a year or more. Committed OKRs are expected to be achieved in the cycle; aspirational OKRs are stretch goals that may span cycles. Scoring options are Andy Grove's yes or no, red, yellow and green, or Google's 0.0 to 1.0 scale averaged across Key Results ([WhatMatters OKR FAQ](https://www.whatmatters.com/faqs/okr-meaning-definition-example)).

**[DURABLE]** OKRs are kept separate from compensation. The mistakes the same source lists are writing business-as-usual goals, confusing OKRs with KPIs (KPIs measure health, OKRs measure change) and sandbagging ([WhatMatters OKR FAQ](https://www.whatmatters.com/faqs/okr-meaning-definition-example)). The often-quoted convention that 0.7 is a good score was not on the page read and is unverified here.

### EOS: the weekly Level 10 meeting

**[VENDOR]** The EOS Level 10 Meeting is weekly, 90 minutes, on the same day and time with the same agenda, starting and ending on time. The agenda is: segue 5 minutes; scorecard 5; rock review 5; customer and employee headlines 5; to-do list 5; identify, discuss, solve (IDS) 60; conclude 5 ([EOS Worldwide, Level 10 Meeting](https://www.eosworldwide.com/blog/level-10-meeting)).

**[VENDOR]** In EOS, "rocks" are 90-day priorities and are reported only as on or off track; anything off track goes to the issues list and is not solved during the review. To-dos from the issue-solving block each have an owner and are due within seven days. Departmental versions can be shorter with the same structure ([EOS Worldwide, Level 10 Meeting](https://www.eosworldwide.com/blog/level-10-meeting)).

### 4DX

**[VENDOR]** FranklinCovey's four disciplines are: focus on the wildly important; act on lead measures, the highest-leverage activities; keep a compelling scoreboard; and create a cadence of accountability through a simple weekly team process that reviews successes, analyses failures and course-corrects ([FranklinCovey, The 4 Disciplines](https://www.franklincovey.com/the-4-disciplines/)). FranklinCovey's statement that more than 80% of strategies fail, and its client results such as an incremental $5.7M in 90 days at Whirlpool, are unsourced on the page and should be treated as marketing.

### Minimum viable operating rhythm

**[INFERENCE]** A minimum operating rhythm for a small SaaS company or an agent department has five tiers. Annually: set three to five company objectives and the budget, and refresh the risk register and the vendor renewals calendar. Quarterly: three to five priorities, each with one owner and a measurable result, plus a retrospective on last quarter's score. Monthly: KPI and financial review, vendor and SaaS spend review, risk register review. Weekly: a 60 to 90 minute fixed-agenda meeting with roughly two-thirds of the time on issue solving, as in the EOS agenda. Daily, optionally: a short written status. The rhythm is the researchers' synthesis.

**[INFERENCE]** KPI scorecard template: five to fifteen weekly numbers, with columns for metric, owner, target, red or green rule and the last 13 weeks. Off-target metrics become issues and are not discussed in the review slot.

**[INFERENCE]** Meeting design rules distilled from the EOS, GitLab and Atlassian sources: a fixed agenda; a pre-read in writing; decisions and to-dos recorded with owner and date, with seven days as the default; and the outcome posted where the work lives. The house style adds Leila Hormozi's five pre-meeting questions from section 2.

### Decision rights: RAPID

**[VENDOR]** In Bain's RAPID model, Recommend drives the process and develops the proposal; Agree confirms feasibility, typically for mandatory matters such as legal or regulatory compliance; Perform owns implementation; Input supplies expertise and surfaces risk; Decide makes the final call, commits the organisation, communicates the rationale and ensures resources ([Bain, RAPID decision making](https://www.bain.com/insights/rapid-decision-making)).

**[VENDOR]** Bain's rules of thumb: ideally one decider and one recommender per decision; if a group decides, define the tie-break up front; use the Agree role sparingly; assign Perform early; place the decider as close to implementation as possible; and start with high-value or high-frequency decisions, because not every decision needs explicit roles. If an Agree holder and the recommender cannot reconcile, the decider makes the call ([Bain, RAPID decision making](https://www.bain.com/insights/rapid-decision-making)).

### Decision rights and records: DACI

**[VENDOR]** In Atlassian's DACI play, the Driver gathers stakeholders and information and gets the decision made by the agreed date; the Approver is one person who makes the final decision; Contributors have a voice but no vote; the Informed are told the outcome ([Atlassian Team Playbook, DACI](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/daci)).

**[VENDOR]** The DACI decision record has these fields: a title phrased as the question; roles; status, impact, due date and outcome; one to two sentences of background; relevant data; decision factors such as cost, time and scalability; an options table with pros, cons and estimated cost; action items with owners; and the final outcome, recorded so later teams understand the reasoning ([Atlassian Team Playbook, DACI](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/daci)).

### RACI for recurring responsibility

**[DURABLE]** A RACI chart lists tasks or deliverables on one axis and roles on the other. Responsible are those who do the work, at least one per task. Accountable is the one person ultimately answerable, who delegates and signs off. Consulted are people whose input is sought, in two-way communication. Informed are people kept up to date, one way. The source is Wikipedia's entry citing the PMBOK Guide (7th edition, 2021, section 4.6.6); PMI itself was not read. The entry presents the single-accountable rule as the position of some project-management theories and not as universal, and lists variants including DACI, RASCI, RACIQ, RACI-VSL, RACIO, PARIS and RACI+F ([Wikipedia, Responsibility assignment matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responsibility_assignment_matrix)).

**[INFERENCE]** The defensible house rule for RACI is exactly one Accountable per row and at least one Responsible, stated as the company's own convention with the PMBOK reference. Use RACI for recurring task responsibility and DACI or RAPID for one-off decisions.

### Decision record template

**[INFERENCE]** A decision record that merges DACI and RAPID has these fields: ID and date; the question; the decider (one name); the recommender or driver; agree roles, only legal, security or finance where mandatory; inputs consulted; options with cost and risk; the decision and rationale; who performs and by when; a review date; and status (proposed, decided, superseded). Decisions live in decision records, not in chat.

**[GAP]** Not verified in this research: Scaling Up (Verne Harnish, the Rockefeller Habits, the daily huddle and the one-page strategic plan); architecture decision record conventions; EOS quarterly and annual session formats; OKR scoring conventions beyond the page read; annual planning cycles; PMI's own RACI page and criticisms of RACI.
