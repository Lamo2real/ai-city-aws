**When to use this:** When setting or reviewing strategy, choosing a segment, positioning, analysing competitors, deciding build, buy or partner, or judging product-market fit.

**Source section:** 5. Product strategy

### Strategy is a leader-owned choice of a few problems

**[DURABLE; practitioner opinion]** In the SVPG model, product strategy is the choice of the few most important problems to solve in service of a customer-centric vision. Its principles are focus, being powered by insights (from data, customers, technology and the industry), transparency, and placing bets, meaning a quarterly portfolio of bets against annual business objectives. Product leaders usually make these calls, not a committee of stakeholders, and teams then receive problems and outcomes instead of feature lists ([SVPG, The Product Operating Model, Feb 2023, modified Jul 2024](https://svpg.com/the-product-operating-model-an-introduction)). **[HOUSE STYLE; primary transcript]** Hormozi's focus rule points the same way: strategy is choosing what to refuse ([Hormozi, The Biggest Mistake Everyone is Making, third-party transcript, video dated 16 Aug 2021](https://rosetta.to/u/alexhormozi/the-biggest-mistake-everyone-is-making)).

### A strategy one-pager for the manager agent

**[INFERENCE]** The manager agent keeps product strategy on one page with seven parts: the vision in one paragraph; the target segment and the problem it has; the two to four problems chosen for this period and the insight behind each; what is explicitly not being done; one outcome metric per problem; the bets and the evidence level of each; and the principles for trade-offs. The outline is the researchers' synthesis from SVPG's strategy principles ([SVPG, The Product Operating Model, Feb 2023, modified Jul 2024](https://svpg.com/the-product-operating-model-an-introduction)) and is not a published template.

### Segment choice: screen with the four indicators

**[INFERENCE; adapts HOUSE STYLE]** For segment choice the department screens each candidate segment against Hormozi's four indicators (real pain, stable or growing, easy to reach, money to spend; [Hormozi, The Key To Finding a Profitable Market, third-party transcript, video dated 21 Jan 2021](https://rosetta.to/u/alexhormozi/the-key-to-finding-a-profitable-market)) and then refines the choice with the positioning method below, which finds the segment that cares most about the product's differentiated value ([April Dunford, A Quickstart Guide to Positioning, 15 Mar 2021](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning)). **[GAP]** Market sizing methods and formal segmentation methods were not researched; no sourced guidance is recorded.

### Positioning in five components, worked in order

**[DURABLE; practitioner opinion]** April Dunford's positioning method (15 March 2021) has five components: competitive alternatives, differentiated capabilities, customer value, the segment that cares most, and market category. The work is done in that order. Begin by asking what a customer would do if the product did not exist; the answer is often a spreadsheet, a manual process or nothing. Then list what the product has that those alternatives lack, ask "so what" of each attribute to reach the value, find who cares most about that value, and only then pick the market category that makes the value obvious ([April Dunford, A Quickstart Guide to Positioning, 15 Mar 2021](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning)).

**[DURABLE; practitioner opinion]** Dunford's practical rules for positioning are: do not rely on a fill-in-the-blank positioning statement; position only against alternatives that actually show up in deals and not against "phantom" rivals; do not assume a new category is needed, because creating one is slow and costly; and test the positioning in customer conversations ([April Dunford, A Quickstart Guide to Positioning, 15 Mar 2021](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning)).

### Competitive analysis starts from the customer's real alternatives

**[DURABLE; practitioner opinion]** The only sourced competitive-analysis guidance in this research is Dunford's: the competitive set is defined by the customer's real alternatives, including the status quo, and other vendors are watched as future threats until they appear in deals ([April Dunford, A Quickstart Guide to Positioning, 15 Mar 2021](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning)). Jobs-to-be-done theory widens the set beyond the product category: in the Christensen Institute's milkshake case, the morning rivals were bananas and bagels ([Christensen Institute, Jobs to Be Done, page modified 30 May 2025](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/)). **[GAP]** Competitive-analysis frameworks beyond this (feature matrices, win-loss analysis, battlecards) were not researched.

### Build, buy or partner

**[VENDOR; practitioner opinion]** Pragmatic Institute, a training company, gives these criteria (page modified 12 September 2025): buy when existing parts can be added without reinventing them; build when nothing similar exists and the capability would become part of the company's core; partner when both companies and the customer gain. The decision should rest on market need. Its three steps are to define the problem through market and competitive analysis, define the ideal solution with the architect, lead engineer or chief technology officer, and then choose the route ([Pragmatic Institute, Buy, Build or Partner, page modified 12 Sep 2025](https://pragmaticinstitute.com/product/framework/buy-build-or-partner)). **[INFERENCE]** A sharper SaaS reading by the researchers: build what differentiates the product in the positioning exercise, buy or integrate commodity capability, and partner where the customer's job spans two products. **[GAP]** No source with quantified criteria (total cost of ownership, time-to-market thresholds) was read; a Forrester framework exists but is paywalled ([Forrester, The Build, Buy, Or Partner Decision Framework, title only, paywalled](https://www.forrester.com/report/the-build-buy-or-partner-decision-framework/RES173053)). Platform and ecosystem strategy was not researched.

### Product-market fit: the Sean Ellis survey protocol

**[DURABLE; practitioner opinion]** The Sean Ellis test asks users how they would feel if they could no longer use the product, with three answers: very disappointed, somewhat disappointed, not disappointed. The 40% threshold comes from Ellis benchmarking close to 100 startups; those at or above 40% "very disappointed" mostly had traction and those below mostly struggled. It is one practitioner's benchmark and not a peer-reviewed result ([First Round Review, How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product Market Fit, 12 Nov 2018](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)). Mixpanel, an analytics vendor, repeats more than 40% as the conventional threshold ([Mixpanel, How to find PMF with data, Jul 2024, edited Jan 2025](https://mixpanel.com/blog/how-to-find-product-market-fit-with-data/)). Ellis's own original post was not fetched; the protocol is recorded through Rahul Vohra's 2018 account.

**[DURABLE; practitioner opinion]** The protocol in Vohra's account of the Sean Ellis test: ask users who used the product at least twice in the past two weeks; ask each user once; about 40 responses give a directional read; and add three open questions on who would benefit most, the main benefit, and how to improve the product ([First Round Review, How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product Market Fit, 12 Nov 2018](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)). Superhuman, a single-company case, scored 22% in summer 2017, 33% after narrowing to the segment that loved it, and 58% about three quarters later ([First Round Review, How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product Market Fit, 12 Nov 2018](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)).

**[DURABLE; practitioner opinion]** Vohra's method for acting on Sean Ellis survey results: segment by answer and build the target profile from the "very disappointed" group; ignore the "not disappointed" group; among the "somewhat disappointed", listen to those whose main benefit matches the fans'; split the roadmap half on deepening what fans love and half on removing what holds the middle group back; re-survey continuously and make the score a team key result ([First Round Review, How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product Market Fit, 12 Nov 2018](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)).

### Product-market fit: the retention curve

**[VENDOR]** Mixpanel's guidance (July 2024, edited January 2025) is that a retention curve that flattens signals product-market fit; its illustration is 50% of a cohort active at day 5 and still 50% at day 20. Once the curve is flat, the job is to raise the plateau. It lists habit-moment examples attributed to Reforge: Slack, messages sent on 4 of 7 days; Zoom, 4 meetings hosted in the first 28 days; Pinterest, a first pin within 7 days and then weekly pinning ([Mixpanel, How to find PMF with data, Jul 2024, edited Jan 2025](https://mixpanel.com/blog/how-to-find-product-market-fit-with-data/)). **[PLATFORM CLAIM]** Mixpanel reports that getting new users to share insights with colleagues flattened its own retention curve, with a claimed threefold effect on six-month retention ([Mixpanel, How to find PMF with data, Jul 2024, edited Jan 2025](https://mixpanel.com/blog/how-to-find-product-market-fit-with-data/)).

### A product-market fit routine for a small SaaS

**[INFERENCE]** A workable product-market fit routine for a small B2B SaaS company: survey recently active users once each; wait for roughly 40 or more responses; report the "very disappointed" share by segment; treat 40% as a rule of thumb and not a law; treat any result from fewer than 40 responses as qualitative; and require a flattening logo and revenue retention curve before scaling acquisition. The routine combines the Ellis protocol ([First Round Review, How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product Market Fit, 12 Nov 2018](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)) and the retention-curve signal ([Mixpanel, How to find PMF with data, Jul 2024, edited Jan 2025](https://mixpanel.com/blog/how-to-find-product-market-fit-with-data/)) and is the researchers' synthesis.

### Strategy pressures reported in 2025 to 2026

**[FAST-MOVING, Q4 2025 fieldwork, published May 2026; survey]** In the PLA/ProductPlan survey of about 250 respondents, fully product-led organisations name market and competitive pressure as their top challenge (45.1%), and 27% of respondents blame missing discovery or customer insight for misalignment between strategy and roadmap ([Product-Led Alliance statistics page citing the PLA/ProductPlan State of Product Management 2026, published May 2026](https://www.productledalliance.com/product-management-statistics/)).

### Sweden and EU: the Data Act and lock-in assumptions

**[FAST-MOVING, applies from 12 Sep 2025; checked on the Commission's policy page, regulation text not read]** The EU Data Act has applied since 12 September 2025. According to the Commission's summary it gives customers a framework for switching between data-processing (cloud) providers and restricts unfair contract terms ([European Commission, Data Act policy page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/data-act)). **[INFERENCE]** For a Swedish SaaS company selling in the EU, switching rights weaken lock-in as a retention strategy, so retention has to come from delivered value. **[GAP]** The phase-out dates for switching charges are not on the Commission summary page; check Regulation (EU) 2023/2854 directly. This is orientation and not legal advice.
