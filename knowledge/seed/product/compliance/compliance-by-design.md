**Source section:** 18. Compliance by design

### Scope and status of this section

**[INFERENCE]** This section covers the EU and Swedish rules that shape interface design directly: deceptive-design and consent rules, the consumer withdrawal function, AI Act Article 50, the planned Digital Fairness Act, and guidance on minors. Every paragraph states how the legal point was verified. The whole section is orientation as of 11 November 2026 and not legal advice; agents escalate to Legal before acting on any point marked secondary or unverified.

### GDPR and deceptive design: the EDPB's six families

**[DURABLE; checked on primary source, EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 version 2.0, adopted 14 Feb 2023]** The European Data Protection Board names six families of deceptive design patterns. Overloading: continuous prompting, privacy maze, too many options. Skipping: deceptive snugness, look over there. Stirring: emotional steering, hidden in plain sight. Obstructing: dead end, longer than necessary, misleading action. Fickle: lacking hierarchy, decontextualising, inconsistent interface, language discontinuity. Left in the dark: conflicting information, ambiguous wording ([EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 v2.0, adopted 14 Feb 2023 (PDF)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-02/edpb_03-2022_guidelines_on_deceptive_design_patterns_in_social_media_platform_interfaces_v2_en_0.pdf)). The guidelines are written for social media interfaces and are listed as final ([EDPB, Guidelines 03/2022 page](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-032022-deceptive-design-patterns-social-media_en)).

**[DURABLE; checked on primary source]** In the EDPB guidelines, fairness under GDPR Article 5(1)(a) is the umbrella test; the consent rules in Article 7 require withdrawal to be about as easy as giving consent; Article 12 requires clear, plain, easily accessible information; and Article 25, data protection by design and by default, is engaged when the most invasive options are pre-selected ([EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 v2.0, adopted 14 Feb 2023 (PDF)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-02/edpb_03-2022_guidelines_on_deceptive_design_patterns_in_social_media_platform_interfaces_v2_en_0.pdf)). **[GAP]** The text of GDPR Article 25 and EDPB Guidelines 4/2019 on data protection by design were not read.

**[DURABLE; checked on primary source]** EDPB best practices that translate into design: shortcuts to the relevant setting wherever related information appears; a contact point and a link to the supervisory authority; a collapsible table of contents in the privacy policy; dated previous versions with changes highlighted; consistent wording; plain-language definitions; contextual just-in-time notices; privacy onboarding early in use; and concrete examples of processing ([EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 v2.0, adopted 14 Feb 2023 (PDF)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-02/edpb_03-2022_guidelines_on_deceptive_design_patterns_in_social_media_platform_interfaces_v2_en_0.pdf)). The EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce published its report on 18 January 2023 ([EDPB, Report of the Cookie Banner Taskforce, 18 Jan 2023](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/other/report-work-undertaken-cookie-banner-taskforce_en)); its contents were not read.

### A consent interface checklist

**[INFERENCE]** The department's consent interface checklist, derived from the EDPB families ([EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 v2.0, adopted 14 Feb 2023 (PDF)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-02/edpb_03-2022_guidelines_on_deceptive_design_patterns_in_social_media_platform_interfaces_v2_en_0.pdf)): equal visual weight for accept and refuse; no pre-selected invasive options; no repeated prompting after refusal; one language throughout; the same wording in the banner and the policy; and withdrawal reachable in the same number of steps as consent. The checklist is the researchers' synthesis.

### Sweden: cookie consent and analytics

**[FAST-MOVING, law-firm guide undated, refers to 2023 events; secondary source only]** According to a law-firm country guide, the Swedish cookie rule is in the Electronic Communications Act (2022:482), chapter 9, section 28. Consent is required for any cookie or similar technology not necessary for a service the user expressly requested; necessary cookies need no consent but still need information. Analytics cookies get no exemption and are grouped with advertising and social media cookies as requiring consent. Consent must meet the GDPR standard: no implied consent from browsing, no pre-ticked boxes, no cookie walls, and withdrawal as easy as giving consent ([Bird & Bird, Global Cookie Review, Sweden, undated, refers to 2023 events](https://cm.twobirds.com/en/trending-topics/global-cookie-review/sweden)).

**[FAST-MOVING, 2023 events; secondary source only]** The same guide says supervision in Sweden is split: PTS enforces the cookie placement rule and IMY supervises where cookie use involves personal data processing. In 2023 PTS reviewed four large organisations and found all in breach, mainly on the validity of consent; PTS's position is that rejecting must be as easy as accepting and offered in the same layer, and that colour or contrast must not be used to favour acceptance. No fine amounts are reported ([Bird & Bird, Global Cookie Review, Sweden, undated, refers to 2023 events](https://cm.twobirds.com/en/trending-topics/global-cookie-review/sweden)). PTS's 2023 supervision documents name Tele2 and the Public Health Agency among those reviewed (primary documents confirmed by title only; [PTS notice to Tele2, 2023, title only](https://www.pts.se/globalassets/startpage/dokument/legala-dokument/beslut/2023/internet/underrattelse-beslutad-tele2.pdf); [PTS notice to Folkhälsomyndigheten, 2023, title only](https://www.pts.se/globalassets/startpage/dokument/legala-dokument/beslut/2023/internet/underrattelse-beslutad-folkhalsomyndigheten.pdf)).

**[INFERENCE]** Product analytics in Sweden: any analytics that stores or reads data on the user's device needs prior opt-in consent with an equally prominent reject option on the first layer. Server-side or first-party measurement that reads nothing from the device may fall outside the cookie rule but still falls under the GDPR; this is the researchers' reading and needs counsel. **[GAP]** PTS's and IMY's own cookie guidance pages were not fetched; whether the EU Digital Omnibus changes cookie consent, whether Swedish supervision has been reformed since 2023, and the GDPR lawful basis for product analytics beyond cookies are unknown here.

### Digital Services Act Article 25

**[DURABLE; secondary source only, text read on an unofficial mirror and not on EUR-Lex]** Article 25 of the Digital Services Act says providers of online platforms may not design, organise or operate interfaces in ways that deceive or manipulate users or materially impair free and informed decisions. The ban does not apply to practices already covered by the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive or the GDPR. Examples on which the Commission may give guidance: making some choices more prominent when asking for a decision; repeatedly asking for a choice already made, such as through pop-ups; and making termination harder than subscription ([DSA Article 25, unofficial mirror](https://www.eu-digital-services-act.com/Digital_Services_Act_Article_25.html)).

**[INFERENCE]** Article 25 binds online platforms, meaning services that store and publicly disseminate user content, so a typical SaaS tool is probably outside it, and consent or sales manipulation is instead caught by the GDPR and the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive. The department treats the three Article 25 examples as a design standard anyway; they also match the expected themes of the Digital Fairness Act. **[GAP]** Article 25 and the small-enterprise exemption were not verified on EUR-Lex, and the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive guidance of December 2021 was not read.

### The withdrawal function: EU rule

**[FAST-MOVING, applies from 19 Jun 2026; secondary source only, law-firm commentary]** Directive (EU) 2023/2673 adds Article 11a to the Consumer Rights Directive; transposition was due by 19 December 2025 and the rules apply from 19 June 2026. The scope is distance contracts concluded through an online interface where a statutory withdrawal right exists, including digital subscriptions and SaaS sold online to consumers, and it applies to any trader targeting EU consumers; contracts with no withdrawal right are outside it ([Taylor Wessing, Feb 2026](https://www.taylorwessing.com/de/insights-and-events/insights/2026/02/withdrawal-button-as-compliance-risk-for-eu-and-non-eu-businesses)).

**[FAST-MOVING, 2026; secondary source only]** Design requirements for the withdrawal function, per law-firm commentary: a function labelled "withdraw from contract here" or an unambiguous equivalent, prominently displayed, easily accessible and available throughout the withdrawal period. It works in two steps: the consumer gives or confirms name, contract identification and means of communication, then submits through a function labelled "confirm withdrawal" or equivalent, and where several contracts exist the consumer must be able to select which one. The trader must send an acknowledgement on a durable medium such as email without undue delay, including the content of the withdrawal and the date and time received ([Greenberg Traurig, May 2026](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/5/eu-consumer-law-new-withdrawal-button-requirements-for-online-contracts)).

**[FAST-MOVING, 2026; secondary source only]** Further points on the withdrawal function from law-firm commentary: no forced registration, authentication or app download to reach the function; it must be visibly distinct from a subscription-cancellation button; and consumers must be told before purchase that the function exists and where it is ([Reed Smith, 20 Jan 2026](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102m2ba/one-click-to-withdraw-why-the-new-withdrawal-button-changes-online-contracts/)). One firm reads placement behind a login as tolerated only where the contract can only be concluded through a customer account, and says vague labels such as "Cancel" are likely insufficient and that hidden placement or friction may be treated as dark patterns ([Taylor Wessing, Feb 2026](https://www.taylorwessing.com/de/insights-and-events/insights/2026/02/withdrawal-button-as-compliance-risk-for-eu-and-non-eu-businesses)).

**[CONTESTED; secondary source only]** Law firms disagree on the German sanction cap for the withdrawal function. One reports that the withdrawal period can extend from 14 days to 12 months and 14 days if consumers are not properly informed, with fines up to EUR 50,000 or up to 4% of annual turnover ([Reed Smith, 20 Jan 2026](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102m2ba/one-click-to-withdraw-why-the-new-withdrawal-button-changes-online-contracts/)); another states a maximum of EUR 2 million or 4% ([Greenberg Traurig, May 2026](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/5/eu-consumer-law-new-withdrawal-button-requirements-for-online-contracts)). These are reports about Germany and not about Sweden.

### The withdrawal function: Swedish transposition

**[FAST-MOVING, 15 Jun 2026; secondary source only, one law firm]** According to a law-firm update, Sweden added the withdrawal function to the Distance and Off-Premises Contracts Act (2005:59), implementing Directive (EU) 2023/2673, with application from 19 June 2026. The design requirements it reports: a clearly labelled, easy-to-read function, with "ångra avtalet här" given as example wording; easy to reach on the same site or app where the contract was made; available throughout the withdrawal period; and it may sit behind a login ([CMS, Ny "ångerknapp" krävs på e-handelsplattformar, 15 Jun 2026](https://cms.law/sv/swe/legal-updates/ny-aangerknapp-kraevs-paa-e-handelsplattformar-fraan-den-19-juni-2026)).

**[FAST-MOVING, 15 Jun 2026; secondary source only]** The Swedish flow reported by the same firm: the consumer gives a name, identifies the contract and chooses how to receive confirmation; a second explicit confirmation step prevents accidental withdrawal; and the business sends an acknowledgement on a durable medium without undue delay, stating when the withdrawal was received. Pre-contract information must say that the function exists and where. The scope is consumer distance contracts, including subscriptions, apps and digital platforms, and no new withdrawal right is created. Non-compliance can be treated as unfair marketing, with injunctions under penalty of a fine and a market disruption fee possible ([CMS, Ny "ångerknapp" krävs på e-handelsplattformar, 15 Jun 2026](https://cms.law/sv/swe/legal-updates/ny-aangerknapp-kraevs-paa-e-handelsplattformar-fraan-den-19-juni-2026)). The government bill is Proposition 2025/26:84 and the committee report is CU11 (primary documents confirmed by title only; [Riksdagen, Prop. 2025/26:84, title only](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/HD0384); [Riksdagen, Civilutskottets betänkande CU11, title only](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/HD01CU11)).

**[CONTESTED; secondary source only]** The law-firm sources differ on login: the Swedish update says the function may sit behind a login ([CMS, Ny "ångerknapp" krävs på e-handelsplattformar, 15 Jun 2026](https://cms.law/sv/swe/legal-updates/ny-aangerknapp-kraevs-paa-e-handelsplattformar-fraan-den-19-juni-2026)), while commentary on the directive says no forced registration or authentication ([Reed Smith, 20 Jan 2026](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102m2ba/one-click-to-withdraw-why-the-new-withdrawal-button-changes-online-contracts/)) or tolerates login only where the contract required an account ([Taylor Wessing, Feb 2026](https://www.taylorwessing.com/de/insights-and-events/insights/2026/02/withdrawal-button-as-compliance-risk-for-eu-and-non-eu-businesses)). **[GAP]** The Swedish statute text, the exact mandated label wording and Konsumentverket's guidance were not read; all Swedish detail is from one law firm. Legal must confirm the label and the login question before build.

### A build checklist for the withdrawal function

**[INFERENCE]** The department's build checklist for the withdrawal function: a persistent, clearly labelled entry point in the footer and on the order or account page, reachable without login where sign-up did not need an account; a first step pre-filled where possible, with a contract selector; a second step with a "confirm withdrawal" button; an automatic timestamped email receipt; updated pre-contract information and terms; and a separate, differently labelled subscription-cancellation flow. For B2B-only SaaS the function is not triggered, because it follows the consumer withdrawal right, but any self-serve plan that consumers or sole traders acting privately can buy brings it into scope. The checklist is the researchers' synthesis from the law-firm sources ([Greenberg Traurig, May 2026](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/5/eu-consumer-law-new-withdrawal-button-requirements-for-online-contracts); [CMS, Ny "ångerknapp" krävs på e-handelsplattformar, 15 Jun 2026](https://cms.law/sv/swe/legal-updates/ny-aangerknapp-kraevs-paa-e-handelsplattformar-fraan-den-19-juni-2026)).

### AI Act Article 50: disclosure and marking

**[FAST-MOVING, Jul-Sep 2026; secondary source only, law-firm summaries of the Act, the Commission's guidelines and the Code of Practice]** Article 50 obligations have applied since 2 August 2026 whether or not a system is high-risk ([Houthoff, 1 Sep 2026](https://www.houthoff.com/insights/news/ai-act-transparency-obligations-and-the-ai-omnibus-regulation/)). Under Article 50(1), systems that interact directly with people must make their artificial nature apparent; hiding this in terms of service, using a generic label such as "assistant", or relying on metadata alone is not enough. AI agents must disclose both that they are AI and whom they act for, at the point of contact and again at key steps: authorisation, reporting, validation and each new interaction ([Faegre Drinker, 30 Jul 2026](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/7/eu-ai-act-commission-confirms-transparency-code-of-practice-as-adequate-and-publishes-final-version-of-its-guidelines-on-transparency-obligations)). An unofficial reproduction of Article 50 adds that disclosure must come by the first interaction unless the AI nature is obvious ([AI Act Article 50, unofficial reproduction at artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)).

**[FAST-MOVING, Jul-Sep 2026; secondary source only]** Under Article 50(2), providers mark outputs in a machine-readable, detectable way; the Code of Practice describes a layered approach, generally signed metadata plus imperceptible watermarking, with lighter rules for free-form text. Systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 have until 2 December 2026 to meet this marking duty ([Faegre Drinker, 30 Jul 2026](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/7/eu-ai-act-commission-confirms-transparency-code-of-practice-as-adequate-and-publishes-final-version-of-its-guidelines-on-transparency-obligations); [Houthoff, 1 Sep 2026](https://www.houthoff.com/insights/news/ai-act-transparency-obligations-and-the-ai-omnibus-regulation/)).

**[FAST-MOVING, Jul 2026; secondary source only]** Under Article 50(4), deepfakes and AI-generated text on matters of public interest need a clear visible label at first exposure; the Commission's standard icon satisfies this, and audio-only formats need a spoken or written disclaimer. An editorial exception applies only with genuine editorial responsibility. Under Article 50(3), deployers of emotion recognition or biometric categorisation must inform the people exposed. The Code of Practice is voluntary and not a safe harbour; fines reach EUR 15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover; and the Act applies where outputs are intended for use in the EU ([Faegre Drinker, 30 Jul 2026](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/7/eu-ai-act-commission-confirms-transparency-code-of-practice-as-adequate-and-publishes-final-version-of-its-guidelines-on-transparency-obligations)). **[GAP]** Article 50, the Commission guidelines and the Code of Practice were not read in the original; the icon design and exact label wording are not recorded, and Swedish supervisory arrangements for the AI Act were not researched.

### An AI disclosure checklist

**[INFERENCE]** The department's AI disclosure checklist: say "AI" plainly in the first message or at the entry point of any chat or agent; name the company the agent acts for; repeat the disclosure at approval and reporting steps; label AI-generated media visibly and keep provenance metadata intact through export; and meet the 2 December 2026 marking date for features launched before August 2026. The checklist is the researchers' synthesis from the law-firm summaries ([Faegre Drinker, 30 Jul 2026](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/7/eu-ai-act-commission-confirms-transparency-code-of-practice-as-adequate-and-publishes-final-version-of-its-guidelines-on-transparency-obligations); [Houthoff, 1 Sep 2026](https://www.houthoff.com/insights/news/ai-act-transparency-obligations-and-the-ai-omnibus-regulation/)).

### The Digital Fairness Act: a reported draft, not law

**[FAST-MOVING, 11 Nov 2026; EU institution page and press]** The European Parliament's tracker describes the Digital Fairness Act as not yet proposed, planned for the fourth quarter of 2026 in the Commission's 2026 work programme, with the public consultation closed on 24 October 2025 (checked on the institution's page, which shows no update date; [European Parliament Legislative Train, Digital Fairness Act](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-protecting-our-democracy-upholding-our-values/file-digital-fairness-act)). The 2025 consultation drew 4,324 valid responses according to a law-firm summary ([Heuking, 8 May 2026](https://www.heuking.de/en/news-events/newsletter-articles/detail/digital-fairness-act-implementation-obligations-companies-need-to-prepare-for.html)). Press reported on 29 September 2026 that the Commission had tentatively listed the act for its 11 November meeting ([MLex, 29 Sep 2026](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/data-privacy-security/articles/2531216)), and on 9 November 2026 MLex published the draft law, with presentation scheduled for Wednesday 11 November and subject to change ([MLex, 9 Nov 2026](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2547809)).

**[FAST-MOVING, 9 Nov 2026; press report, secondary source only]** According to a press report relaying Reuters, the draft Digital Fairness Act would restrict manipulative interface design, require platforms to let users switch off engagement-maximising features, ban personalised advertising aimed at children and vulnerable consumers, require disclosure of the stock remaining at an advertised price, and allow fines up to 6% of worldwide annual turnover; Parliament and member states must still approve it ([PYMNTS/CPI relaying Reuters, 9 Nov 2026](https://www.pymnts.com/cpi-posts/eu-draft-bill-targets-unfair-digital-practices-with-fines-of-up-to-6/)). The earlier law-firm summary listed expected themes as manipulative design (pre-selected paid add-ons, fake countdown timers, cancellation harder than sign-up), addictive design and virtual currencies, simple cancellation of digital contracts, transparency of personalised pricing and recommendations, and influencer marketing ([Heuking, 8 May 2026](https://www.heuking.de/en/news-events/newsletter-articles/detail/digital-fairness-act-implementation-obligations-companies-need-to-prepare-for.html)).

**[INFERENCE] [GAP]** The department treats the Digital Fairness Act as "draft reported, formal proposal expected 11 November 2026, not yet law". Whether the Commission actually adopted the proposal on 11 November 2026 is unconfirmed, because no source dated on or after that day was read, and draft details on subscription cancellation and AI chatbots were not in the fetched reports. Even after a proposal, negotiation in Parliament and Council normally takes well over a year, so no obligations apply in 2026. Agents check the Commission's press corner for the adopted text before stating its contents as final.

### Minors and age-appropriate design

**[FAST-MOVING, 14 Jul 2025; checked on the Commission's page]** The Commission's guidelines on protecting minors under the Digital Services Act recommend private accounts by default for minors; turning off by default the features that drive excessive use, such as streaks, autoplay, read receipts, push notifications and disappearing content; safeguards on built-in AI chatbots; age assurance that is accurate, robust, non-intrusive and non-discriminatory; and recommender systems that favour explicit signals over behavioural ones ([European Commission, Guidelines on the protection of minors, 14 Jul 2025](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/commission-publishes-guidelines-protection-minors)). **[GAP]** Swedish guidance on age-appropriate design was not researched.

### Compliance controls must themselves be accessible

**[INFERENCE]** Withdrawal, consent and AI-label components must themselves meet WCAG 2.2 AA, including focus not obscured by banners and 24-pixel targets ([W3C WAI, What's New in WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/)), because a compliance control that is not accessible fails two regimes at once.
