# Ship small, gate hard, prove everything

As of 11 November 2026, the foundational knowledge for an AI-run Application department is a short list of durable engineering habits wrapped in controls that the platform enforces and the law now demands evidence for. The durable habits are small changes merged to trunk at least daily, automated tests that answer in under ten minutes, peer review answered within one business day, a modular monolith with pooled tenancy and database-enforced tenant isolation, schema and API changes made through expand, migrate and contract, and progressive rollout with alarm-driven automatic rollback. The evidence on AI coding agents is lopsided: use is near-universal (90% in DORA's 2025 survey and in JetBrains' 2026 survey), organisational throughput is up, but delivery instability is still up too, a vendor benchmark finds only 55% of AI-generated samples free of a known vulnerability class with no improvement in two years, CircleCI's telemetry shows main-branch success down to 70.8%, and no trustworthy measurement of individual speed-up exists. The consistent answer from the UK NCSC, the Five Eyes agencies, OWASP, GitHub, Anthropic and OpenAI is that anything which must always or never happen belongs in a deterministic control (sandbox, default-deny egress, missing credentials, branch protection, human merge) and never in an instruction file. For a Sweden-based SaaS company the binding dates cluster tightly: Cyber Resilience Act reporting has applied since 11 September 2026 to any installable product, the new Product Liability Directive treats software including SaaS as a product from 9 December 2026 (the Swedish bill proposes 1 January 2027), Data Act switching charges fall to zero on 12 January 2027, and the Swedish Cybersäkerhetslag has been in force since 15 January 2026 with Post- och telestyrelsen supervising cloud and managed service providers. Customers and auditors increasingly ask for machine-verifiable proof of all this (SBOMs, scan logs, named human approvers, agent session logs), and the SOC 2 criteria are being revised to separate in-house software change management and add AI points of focus. The research behind this document ran out of search and fetch budget several times, so penetration-test frequency, ISO/IEC 27001 control wording, SOC 2 criterion text, estimation and method comparisons, preview environments, the EU AI Act and several Swedish authority designations remain unverified and are listed as gaps instead of being filled from memory.

## How to read and split this document

This document is written to be cut into atomic knowledge-base entries for a vector index and a graph database. Every body paragraph is meant to stand alone: it opens with one or more labels, names its subject in full, and keeps each number next to its date and its source link. Numbered sections group domains; sub-headings name the single topic of the paragraphs beneath them. Tables repeat their source link in every row so that a row can be split out as its own entry. When splitting, keep the label, the date and the link with the claim.

The labels mean the following. **[DURABLE]** marks a principle, definition or long-standing rule that has held for years and is unlikely to age quickly; numbers inside older durable sources are historical. **[FAST-MOVING, date]** marks a figure, version number, survey result, tool behaviour or legal status that will age, with the date of the source. **[VENDOR]** marks a figure or recommendation from a party with a commercial interest in the conclusion (tool vendors, cloud providers, security vendors, audit and law firms describing their own market), including large telemetry datasets from such companies; treat it as correlational and self-selected. **[PLATFORM CLAIM]** marks a platform's or tool maker's own description of how its product behaves, which nobody has independently verified. **[CONTESTED]** marks a point where sources disagree. **[INFERENCE]** marks a synthesis or default written by the researchers that no single fetched source states; adopt it as a starting position and tune it against your own data. **[GAP]** marks something the research did not verify or could not find. **[HOUSE STYLE]** is reserved for Alex or Leila Hormozi's published frameworks, which the owner has chosen as the default style where they exist; the research notes for this department contain no Hormozi material on software engineering, so the label is not used in the body and no house-style rule should be assumed for engineering work.

For legal, tax and regulatory points a qualifier is added inside or beside the label. "Checked on primary source" means the statute, regulation or official page was read during the research (through an extraction tool, so re-check the exact article or section wording before quoting it). "Secondary source only" means a law firm, audit firm, agency summary or independent site was read and the primary text was not. "Unverified, not fetched" means the point comes from the researchers' background recollection, with a pointer to where it should be confirmed. Agents must not gate a live decision on an unverified legal point without confirmation from the Legal department or outside counsel.

Seven caveats apply to the whole document. First, legal and regulatory content is orientation as of 11 November 2026 and is not professional legal, tax or compliance advice. Second, the shared web-search and page-fetch budgets ran out during all three research passes, so a number of well-known primary sources were never opened; they appear in section 18, not in the body. Third, the default context assumed by the researchers is a Sweden-based SaaS company running on AWS and selling in the EU; general practice is written to fit any company and Swedish or EU specifics are called out. Fourth, the survey and telemetry numbers are largely self-reported or vendor-platform data (DORA is published by Google, Octoverse by GitHub, the State of Software Delivery by CircleCI, the Developer Ecosystem survey by JetBrains) and none is Sweden-specific. Fifth, DORA's most recent annual report is the 2025 edition; no 2026 report was listed on 11 November 2026 ([DORA research](https://dora.dev/research/)). Sixth, all sources are paraphrased, and no source wording should be attributed as a quotation. Seventh, NIST publications bind US federal systems only and are used here as reference standards, not as law in Sweden.

## 1. Delivery lifecycle: small batches reach trunk daily and review answers within a day

### Design records: what an ADR is and when to write one

**[DURABLE]** An Architectural Decision Record captures a single architecturally significant decision together with its rationale, trade-offs and consequences, and a project's collection of ADRs forms its decision log. Michael Nygard's 2011 post popularised the practice, and template families (MADR, Nygard, Y-statements) are catalogued on the community site ([adr.github.io](https://adr.github.io/)). Write an ADR when a decision is hard to reverse or shapes many later changes; skip it for routine implementation choices. **[GAP]** The research found no quantitative evidence on the effectiveness of RFCs or design documents, and the template section lists were not retrieved, so the ADR material here is definitional.

### Design documents are now inputs to coding agents and must be pruned

**[FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026]** Thoughtworks Technology Radar Volume 35 (November 2026) lists semantic review of AI-edited prose in its Assess ring, meaning an LLM checks specifications, ADRs and plans for unintended changes of meaning, which signals that design documents now feed coding agents as well as people ([Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)). The same volume places AI-driven documentation sprawl and agent instruction bloat in its Caution ring: large volumes of unmaintained, contradictory generated documents degrade human and agent work alike, and the remedy is to prune and to convert rules into automated checks ([Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)). Volume 34 (April 2026) placed curated shared instructions for software teams and context engineering in Adopt ([Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf)).

### Trunk-based development: the thresholds to hold

**[DURABLE]** DORA's trunk-based development guidance sets four thresholds: at most three active branches per application repository, a merge to trunk at least once a day, branches that normally live for hours and not days, and no code freezes or integration phases. The branch-count figure derives from DORA's 2016 to 2017 data, so treat it as a durable principle with a historical number ([DORA trunk-based development](https://dora.dev/capabilities/trunk-based-development/)). Use trunk-based development as the default for any product team that deploys continuously; the practice only works when tests run before changes land, because that is what keeps trunk green.

**[DURABLE]** DORA names three pitfalls that break trunk-based development: reviews needing several approvers that take hours or days push developers toward larger batches, asynchronous review combined with task-switching lengthens merge delay, and a trunk without pre-merge tests does not stay releasable ([DORA trunk-based development](https://dora.dev/capabilities/trunk-based-development/)). To measure the practice, count active branches, count and time code freezes, track the percentage of branches merged daily, and track average review approval time while investigating outliers ([DORA trunk-based development](https://dora.dev/capabilities/trunk-based-development/)).

**[FAST-MOVING, Sep 2025]** DORA's 2025 report (published 23 September 2025) names working in small batches and strong version control practices, meaning frequent commits and proficiency with rollback, among the seven capabilities that amplify the benefit of AI assistance ([DORA 2025 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf)). Small batches therefore matter more, not less, when agents write the code.

### Stacked pull requests: fine when each one ships alone

**[CONTESTED]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 35 (November 2026) puts stacked pull requests in Caution because chains of dependent pull requests can hide poor up-front decomposition ([Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)), while Google's engineering practices recommend stacking small changes as one way to split work ([Google small CLs](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/small-cls.html)). **[INFERENCE]** The researchers' resolution is that stacking is acceptable when every pull request in the stack is independently shippable and is a warning sign when the stack exists only because the work was not decomposed first.

### Code review: the approval standard

**[DURABLE]** Google's reviewer standard is to approve once a change clearly improves overall code health even if it is imperfect, and never to approve a change that worsens code health except in an emergency. Optional polish is prefixed with "Nit:", technical facts and data outrank personal preference, the style guide is the authority on style, and a stalled disagreement is escalated instead of left to block the change ([Google review standard](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/standard.html)). Apply this standard to human and agent reviewers alike.

### Code review: speed

**[DURABLE]** Google's guidance on review speed is that one business day is the maximum time to respond to a review request, that a reviewer should not interrupt focused work but should pick the review up at the next natural pause, and that approving with comments is acceptable when remaining remarks are minor or optional ([Google review speed](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/speed.html)).

### Code review: change size

**[DURABLE]** Google's guidance on change size is that a change should be one self-contained unit, that about 100 lines is usually reasonable and about 1,000 lines usually too large (the reviewer decides), that a change spread across many files is harder still, that refactoring belongs in a separate change from behaviour changes, that tests belong in the same change as the logic they cover, and that a reviewer may reject a change for size alone ([Google small CLs](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/small-cls.html)). **[DURABLE]** Google's 2018 case study of modern code review analysed about 9 million reviewed changes, 12 interviews and a 44-person survey; the abstract page does not give size or latency medians, so no such figure is quoted here ([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/modern-code-review-a-case-study-at-google/)).

### Change approval: peer review beats external approval boards

**[DURABLE]** DORA found no evidence that formal external approval, such as a change advisory board or senior sign-off, lowers change fail rate; such approval slows delivery and produces bigger, riskier releases. DORA's recommended replacement is peer review recorded in the development platform as the segregation-of-duties control, automated detection of bad changes, extra scrutiny only for high-risk changes, and a change advisory board repositioned as a coordination body. The finding rests on DORA's 2019 report ([DORA streamlining change approval](https://dora.dev/capabilities/streamlining-change-approval/)). DORA also advises measuring the percentage of changes promoted without manual approval and the time changes wait for external approvers, and making the normal path fast enough that emergencies use it too ([DORA streamlining change approval](https://dora.dev/capabilities/streamlining-change-approval/)).

### Review in an agent-heavy team: tier oversight by risk and avoid approval theatre

**[FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 35 (November 2026) places risk-tiered human oversight in Trial, meaning review depth is matched to change risk and low-risk changes are merged automatically. It places human-in-the-loop theatre (approval steps nobody can realistically evaluate) and the "meat proxy" habit (forwarding unverified LLM output to colleagues) in Caution, and its theme text says review capacity and delivery pipelines are becoming the bottleneck as developers run several agent sessions in parallel ([Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques); [Radar overview](https://www.thoughtworks.com/en-us/radar)). **[INFERENCE]** For a manager agent directing worker agents this implies routing by risk tier: merge low-risk changes automatically when strong automated checks pass, reserve human review for high-risk ones, and never count a rubber-stamp approval as a control.

### Code review as the last manual gate before production

**[VENDOR]** Amazon describes code review as the last manual approval before production and uses a deployment-safety checklist during review that asks about test coverage, monitoring impact and whether the change can be rolled back; the article's publication date was not captured and it describes Amazon's internal practice ([AWS Builders' Library](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)).

### Pair programming: a sensible default, not a proven law

**[DURABLE]** Böckeler and Siessegger (15 January 2020) advise treating pairing as a sensible default and not as dogma, capping it at roughly six hours a day, choosing among driver/navigator, ping-pong (for test-driven development) and strong-style (for knowledge transfer), and setting the work-in-progress limit to the number of pairs. The benefits they claim are knowledge spread, continuous review, focus and faster onboarding; the costs are fatigue, scheduling and power dynamics. The authors explicitly warn against claiming pairing is scientifically proven and recommend local experiments ([On Pair Programming](https://martinfowler.com/articles/on-pair-programming.html)). DORA states that pairing or synchronous review is preferable to asynchronous review for keeping batches small ([DORA trunk-based development](https://dora.dev/capabilities/trunk-based-development/)), and the Radar Volume 34 theme (April 2026) says pair programming, mutation testing and DORA metrics matter more with coding agents ([Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf)).

**[GAP]** No controlled evidence on pair or mob programming effect sizes was opened. A 2009 meta-analysis by Hannay, Dybå, Arisholm and Sjøberg was located but not read ([EBSE bibliography entry](https://ebse.webspace.durham.ac.uk/ebse-bibliography/the-effectiveness-of-pair-programming-a-meta-analysis/)); the researchers' unverified recollection is a small quality gain, shorter duration and higher effort with strong heterogeneity, and those figures must not be published without opening the paper. Nothing was retrieved on mob or ensemble programming.

### Definition of done

**[DURABLE]** DORA's rule is that nobody should declare work development-complete unless automated acceptance tests pass, and that developers should get automated test feedback in under ten minutes both locally and in continuous integration ([DORA test automation](https://dora.dev/capabilities/test-automation/)). Google adds that any change which adds or alters logic ships with new or updated tests in the same change ([Google small CLs](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/small-cls.html)). **[GAP]** No authoritative definition-of-done standard beyond DORA's acceptance-test rule was retrieved; the Scrum Guide was not opened.

**[INFERENCE]** A workable default checklist for a single change combines the sourced elements above: a linked requirement or ADR where the decision is architecturally significant, a single-purpose diff near the 100-line guide, tests included, continuous integration green in under ten minutes, a review response within one business day, a feature flag if the work is incomplete, a merge to trunk the same day, and a stated rollback path. Each element is individually sourced in this section; the combination is the researchers' synthesis.

## 2. Architecture: start modular and monolithic, and treat tenant isolation as its own control

### Monolith first: when to start with a monolith and when microservices fit

**[DURABLE]** Martin Fowler (3 June 2015) observed that successful microservice systems mostly began as monoliths that were split later, and that systems built as microservices from scratch often ran into serious trouble. Start with a monolith when product value is unproven, when speed of feedback matters most, when the system is simple or when boundaries are not yet understood. Microservices fit when complexity justifies the overhead Fowler calls the microservice premium, when the team has prior experience, or when the system replaces one whose boundaries are predictable. Fowler himself says the evidence is thin and the advice tentative, and adds that a monolith only eases later extraction if it is modular in both its APIs and its data storage ([MonolithFirst](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/MonolithFirst.html)).

### Modular monolith: one deployment unit with enforced domain boundaries

**[DURABLE]** Shopify (21 February 2019) defines a modular monolith as one codebase and one deployment unit with strictly enforced boundaries between domains, and chose it over microservices to gain modularity without adding deployment units, network calls and separate pipelines. Shopify enforced boundaries by reorganising code by business domain (orders, shipping, billing) instead of technical layer, by running a continuous-integration tool that flagged calls bypassing a component's public interface and scored each component, and by planning to load only declared dependencies. The costs it reported were about 6,000 classes mapped by hand, one risky large file-move change, and isolation as continuing work ([Shopify Engineering, 21 Feb 2019](https://shopify.engineering/deconstructing-monolith-designing-software-maximizes-developer-productivity)). **[GAP]** This is a single-company account from 2019; no comparative study of modular monoliths against microservices was retrieved.

### Internal platforms are near-universal but carry a small instability cost

**[FAST-MOVING, Sep 2025]** DORA's 2025 report found about 90% of organisations have at least one internal platform and 76% have a dedicated platform team; higher platform quality is associated with better organisational performance and also with a small increase in delivery instability ([DORA 2025 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf)).

### REST API design rules

**[DURABLE]** Microsoft's API design guidance (page last updated 8 May 2025) recommends naming resources with nouns and plural collections, nesting no deeper than collection, item, collection, and not mirroring database tables. PUT must be idempotent while PATCH is not guaranteed to be; a POST that creates a resource returns 201 with a Location header; long-running work returns 202 with a status URL and then 303 to the created resource. Collections are paginated with limit and offset with a default of 25, a maximum limit is enforced as a denial-of-service control, and requested fields are validated against what the caller is allowed to see ([Azure API design](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/best-practices/api-design)).

### API versioning options and parallel change

**[DURABLE]** Microsoft's guidance (updated 8 May 2025) lists five versioning approaches with their trade-offs: additive-only change (the simplest), a version in the URI path (visible and cache-friendly but it changes resource identity), a query-string parameter, a custom header, and a media type (fits hypermedia, needs more server logic and can fragment caches). It recommends contract-first design with OpenAPI ([Azure API design](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/best-practices/api-design)). Parallel change offers an alternative to explicit versioning: widen what the endpoint accepts or returns, or add a new endpoint, migrate consumers, then remove the old form ([ParallelChange, 13 May 2014](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/ParallelChange.html)).

### Tenant identification and correlation IDs in APIs

**[DURABLE]** Microsoft's guidance (updated 8 May 2025) says a multi-tenant API identifies the tenant by subdomain, by a header or token claim, or by URI path, and warns that header-based identification risks cache leakage when caches ignore the header. It also recommends propagating a correlation ID on every request and response for end-to-end tracing ([Azure API design](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/best-practices/api-design)).

### Idempotency keys for unsafe requests

**[VENDOR]** Stripe's reference implementation has the client send an `Idempotency-Key` header holding a random value (a version 4 UUID is suggested, up to 255 characters, with no personal data). The server stores the first status code and body and replays them for retries, including 500 responses; reusing a key with different parameters returns an error; keys may be pruned once they are at least 24 hours old; and only POST needs a key because GET and DELETE are idempotent by definition ([Stripe idempotent requests](https://docs.stripe.com/api/idempotent_requests)). **[INFERENCE]** The 24-hour retention in Stripe's design is a practical lower bound for an idempotency store where client retries can span hours. **[GAP]** IETF work on a standard idempotency header was not retrieved.

### Choosing REST, GraphQL or gRPC

**[VENDOR]** AWS's comparison pages say GraphQL suits limited bandwidth and fewer round trips, combining several data sources behind one endpoint, and clients whose data needs vary widely, while REST suits simpler data, clients that use data in similar ways and cases without complex querying; one application can use both. REST returns whole resources across many URLs so over-fetching and under-fetching are common, whereas GraphQL returns only requested fields from one endpoint, needs a typed server schema and evolves by deprecating fields instead of versioning URLs ([AWS: GraphQL vs REST](https://aws.amazon.com/compare/the-difference-between-graphql-and-rest/)). gRPC fits high-performance, high-volume or streaming communication and internal polyglot microservices whose API changes rarely, while REST fits web architectures and public APIs that outside developers must understand easily. gRPC always runs on HTTP/2 with binary Protocol Buffers, supports client, server and bidirectional streaming and ships code generation, but it couples client and server through a shared proto file, is not human-readable and is less attractive for browsers ([AWS: gRPC vs REST](https://aws.amazon.com/compare/the-difference-between-grpc-and-rest/)). The AWS pages carry no visible date.

**[INFERENCE]** A default for a B2B SaaS that applies those criteria is REST with an OpenAPI contract for the public API, gRPC only between internal services where latency or streaming justifies the coupling, and GraphQL where one front end aggregates many back ends. **[GAP]** No vendor-neutral or measured comparison of the three styles was found, and the AWS pages list no GraphQL drawbacks, so caching complexity, query-cost limits and N+1 resolver problems are unsourced here.

### MCP and server-driven UI as interface choices

**[FAST-MOVING, Apr 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 34 (April 2026) puts "MCP by default" in Caution, on the grounds that a well-designed command-line interface is often enough, and puts server-driven UI in Trial for highly dynamic mobile surfaces as a way to avoid app-store review cycles ([Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf)).

### Transactional outbox: publish events atomically with the database write

**[DURABLE]** The transactional outbox pattern solves the problem of updating a database and sending a message atomically without two-phase commit while preserving order. The service writes the message to an outbox table inside the same local transaction as the business change, and a separate relay publishes it to the broker, implemented either by tailing the transaction log or by a polling publisher. Because the relay can publish a message more than once (for example after a crash between publishing and marking it sent), consumers must be idempotent, typically by recording processed message IDs ([microservices.io: Transactional outbox](https://microservices.io/patterns/data/transactional-outbox.html)).

### Saga: a sequence of local transactions with written compensations

**[DURABLE]** A saga is a sequence of local transactions across services in which a step that fails a business rule causes earlier steps to be undone by explicitly written compensating transactions. Coordination is either choreography, where each service reacts to domain events, or orchestration, where an orchestrator tells participants what to run. Sagas give no automatic rollback and no isolation, so concurrent sagas can produce anomalies that need designed countermeasures; each step must update its database and publish atomically, which is what the outbox or event sourcing provides; and a client that starts a saga synchronously learns the result by polling a status resource or by a notification such as a webhook ([microservices.io: Saga](https://microservices.io/patterns/data/saga.html)). **[INFERENCE]** Any service that both writes its database and emits an event should adopt an outbox with idempotent consumers before adopting sagas, because the saga pattern depends on that atomicity. **[GAP]** The trade-offs between choreography and orchestration are not spelled out on the page read, and schema registries and delivery-semantics guidance were not retrieved.

### Event sourcing and durable execution

**[DURABLE]** DORA lists event sourcing as one way to keep serving during a schema migration, because changes queue as events and are applied afterwards ([DORA database change management](https://dora.dev/capabilities/database-change-management/)). **[FAST-MOVING, Apr 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 34 (April 2026) puts ignoring durability in agent workflows in Caution: workflows that work in development fail in production without durable execution ([Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf)).

### Expand, migrate, contract for every backward-incompatible change

**[DURABLE]** Parallel change (Danilo Sato, 13 May 2014) makes a backward-incompatible change in three phases: expand by adding the new form beside the old, migrate by moving clients over incrementally (usually the longest phase), and contract by removing the old form once nothing uses it. It applies to database refactoring, to remote API evolution as an alternative to versioning, and to canary or blue-green deployment where two versions run side by side. The benefit is that the system is releasable in every phase; the risk is that a contract phase which never happens leaves the codebase worse than before, so the removal needs explicit tracking. A client-side feature flag can control which version is in use during migration ([ParallelChange, 13 May 2014](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/ParallelChange.html)).

**[INFERENCE]** A schema-migration rule for zero-downtime deploys follows from parallel change: every breaking schema change ships as at least three deploys (add the new column or table and dual-write; backfill and switch reads; drop the old structure), each reversible, with a ticket for the contract step created when the expand step merges.

### Database change management

**[DURABLE]** DORA's database guidance is to keep schema changes in version control with application code, write one numbered migration script per change, record which migrations each database instance has applied, test against production-like scrubbed data, and use online schema-change tools (gh-ost, pt-online-schema-change) or expand and contract for zero downtime, while keeping tables small through partitioning and archiving. Schema deployments should be decoupled from application deployments, and the target is 100% of database changes through an automated push-button process. DORA suggests measuring the share of failed changes that involve the database, the lead time added by database work, and the percentage of database changes fully automated ([DORA database change management](https://dora.dev/capabilities/database-change-management/)).

**[VENDOR]** Amazon deploys new code beside old code in a production-like stage specifically to catch data written in a format the previous version cannot read, and confirms during review that each change can be rolled back; the article is undated in the notes and describes Amazon's internal practice ([AWS Builders' Library](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)).

### Multi-tenancy: authentication is not isolation

**[DURABLE]** AWS's SaaS guidance describes a cross-tenant access as potentially unrecoverable for a SaaS business and says the isolation strategy depends on domain, compliance, deployment model and service choice ([AWS SaaS Lens, tenant isolation](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/saas-lens/tenant-isolation.html)). Authentication and authorisation are not isolation: a user can be authenticated and authorised and still reach another tenant's resources, so isolation uses tenant context to scope every resource access. Dedicated stacks can be isolated with coarse network-level policy, while pooled resources such as items in one table need fine-grained policies ([AWS SaaS fundamentals, tenant isolation](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/whitepapers/latest/saas-architecture-fundamentals/tenant-isolation.html)). The AWS pages carry no visible date.

### Multi-tenancy: silo, pool and bridge

**[DURABLE]** AWS treats data partitioning as a separate decision from isolation. In the silo model a tenant gets dedicated resources (a full stack or a database per tenant) while identity, onboarding and operations stay shared; in the pool model tenants share infrastructure for economies of scale, manageability and agility; the bridge model mixes the two, with some services siloed and others pooled. The choice is made per service on regulatory profile, noisy-neighbour behaviour, cost and market factors ([AWS SaaS Lens: silo, pool and bridge](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/saas-lens/silo-pool-and-bridge-models.html)). Choose silo storage when customers or the domain require separate storage, when pooled isolation is not granular enough, or to contain noisy neighbours and meet service-level agreements ([AWS SaaS fundamentals, data partitioning](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/whitepapers/latest/saas-architecture-fundamentals/data-partitioning.html)).

### Multi-tenancy on PostgreSQL: row-level security for pooled tenants

**[VENDOR]** AWS Prescriptive Guidance maps the three models to PostgreSQL isolation units: silo is a database instance per tenant, bridge is a database or schema per tenant on a shared instance, and pool is shared tables with a tenant identifier column ([AWS Prescriptive Guidance: PostgreSQL partitioning models](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/prescriptive-guidance/latest/saas-multitenant-managed-postgresql/partitioning-models.html)). For pooled tenants AWS recommends PostgreSQL row-level security enabled on every table holding tenant data, because that centralises isolation in the database instead of relying on each developer's query filters, and it prefers a policy comparing `tenant_id` with a runtime variable the application sets per request (its example uses `app.current_tenant`) over one database user per tenant ([AWS Prescriptive Guidance: row-level security](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/prescriptive-guidance/latest/saas-multitenant-managed-postgresql/rls.html)).

**[INFERENCE]** The default for a small or mid-sized Swedish SaaS team is a modular monolith on AWS with pooled tenancy, row-level security and a mandatory `tenant_id` on every table, key and policy; move a tenant to bridge or silo only for a contractual, data-residency or noisy-neighbour reason. Fowler, Shopify and AWS support the parts; the combined recommendation is the researchers'. **[GAP]** Row-level-security caveats (table owners and superusers bypassing policies unless forced, and resetting tenant context under connection pooling with RDS Proxy or PgBouncer) are not on the AWS page read, and the SaaS Lens gives no tiering or cost-attribution guidance.

### Twelve-factor application rules

**[DURABLE]** The twelve-factor methodology (page last updated 2017) lists one codebase, explicitly declared dependencies, configuration in the environment, backing services as attached resources, strict separation of build, release and run, stateless processes, port binding, scaling out by process, fast start-up with graceful shutdown, parity between development and production, logs as event streams, and administrative tasks as one-off processes ([12factor.net](https://12factor.net/)).

### Sweden and EU hosting specifics

**[GAP]** The research did not source AWS Stockholm region details, the AWS European Sovereign Cloud, or data-residency choices for a Swedish SaaS company, and no AWS-specific golden-path guidance (Service Catalog, Proton, Backstage on AWS) was retrieved. The Data Act duty to publish the jurisdiction of the infrastructure (section 11) makes the hosting-region decision a published fact, so it should be recorded in an ADR.

## 3. Web front end and mobile release: platform-defined numbers belong in gates

### Core Web Vitals thresholds

**[FAST-MOVING, Oct 2024]** Google's "good" thresholds for Core Web Vitals are Largest Contentful Paint of 2.5 seconds or less, Interaction to Next Paint of 200 milliseconds or less and Cumulative Layout Shift of 0.1 or less, each assessed at the 75th percentile of page loads and split by mobile and desktop; a page passes only when all three pass. The page was last updated on 31 October 2024 ([web.dev Web Vitals](https://web.dev/articles/vitals)). **[GAP]** The "needs improvement" and "poor" bands appear only as images on that page and were not extracted, and no 2026 change to the thresholds was confirmed.

### Measuring Core Web Vitals in the field and in the lab

**[DURABLE]** Field data for Core Web Vitals comes from the Chrome User Experience Report, PageSpeed Insights and Search Console; lab data comes from Lighthouse and browser developer tools. Lighthouse cannot measure Interaction to Next Paint, so Total Blocking Time serves as the lab proxy, and the `web-vitals` JavaScript library collects all three metrics from real users ([web.dev Web Vitals, updated 31 Oct 2024](https://web.dev/articles/vitals)). **[INFERENCE]** A reasonable web performance budget fails continuous integration on lab regressions in Largest Contentful Paint, Cumulative Layout Shift and Total Blocking Time and alerts when field 75th-percentile values cross the "good" thresholds.

### Front-end technology and testing signals from the Radar

**[FAST-MOVING, Apr 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 34 (April 2026) has React, React Native and Svelte in Adopt, axe-core in Adopt for automated accessibility checks in continuous integration, and browser-based component testing (for example with Playwright) in Trial as more reliable than emulated DOMs ([Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf)). **[GAP]** Volume 35 ring assignments for tools, platforms and languages were not retrieved; only its techniques quadrant was.

### iOS phased release

**[PLATFORM CLAIM]** Apple's phased release for iOS runs over seven days at 1%, 2%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 50% and 100% of users who have automatic updates on. It can be paused for a cumulative 30 days with any number of pauses, anyone can still update manually so the percentages are not a hard cap, and the "Release to All Users" action ends the phase early ([App Store Connect help](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/update-your-app/release-a-version-update-in-phases/)). The help page was read in November 2026 and carries no version date in the notes.

### Google Play staged rollout

**[PLATFORM CLAIM]** Google Play staged rollouts apply only to updates and not to first publication, and the percentage never increases automatically. Halting a rollout stops new users receiving the version but those who already have it keep it, the recommended fix for a bad build is a new corrected release, and country targeting cannot be narrowed after the rollout starts ([Play Console help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/6346149)). The help page was read in November 2026.

### Android vitals bad-behaviour thresholds

**[FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026]** Android vitals defines bad-behaviour thresholds of a 1.09% user-perceived crash rate overall and 8% per phone model, a 0.47% user-perceived ANR rate overall and 8% per phone model, and 5% for excessive partial wake locks, assessed on a 28-day average. Exceeding them can reduce an app's visibility on Google Play or add a warning to its store listing, and further thresholds for memory, bitmap memory and code optimisation may affect visibility from February 2027 ([Android vitals](https://developer.android.com/topic/performance/vitals)). The page was read in November 2026.

### Mobile has no true rollback, so risk sits behind server-side flags

**[INFERENCE]** Neither mobile store supports a true rollback, since Apple pauses and Google halts but neither removes an installed build. Risky mobile behaviour should therefore sit behind server-side feature flags, and backend APIs must stay compatible with at least the previous app versions still in the field. The store behaviours are sourced in this section; the design rule is the researchers' synthesis.

## 4. Quality and testing: fast trusted tests, and mutation testing now that agents write tests

### Test automation principles

**[DURABLE]** DORA's test automation guidance makes developers the primary authors and maintainers of acceptance tests, demands feedback in under ten minutes locally and in continuous integration, and holds that ten fast reliable tests beat hundreds that nobody trusts. Flaky tests must not be tolerated; a defect found by a higher-level or exploratory test should produce a new unit test; exploratory and usability testing stay manual; and a brownfield system should begin with a skeleton pipeline of one unit test, one acceptance test and an automated deploy. When a defect reaches production, tests in the pipeline are added or updated ([DORA test automation](https://dora.dev/capabilities/test-automation/)).

### The test pyramid

**[DURABLE]** The test pyramid (Ham Vocke, 26 February 2018) prescribes many fast unit tests, fewer integration tests and very few end-to-end tests on critical journeys. Tests should check observable behaviour and not internals, be pushed as far down the pyramid as possible, and be deleted at higher levels when lower-level tests already cover the same thing; the inverted "ice-cream cone" shape is the anti-pattern ([Practical Test Pyramid](https://martinfowler.com/articles/practical-test-pyramid.html)).

### Consumer-driven contract tests

**[DURABLE]** In consumer-driven contract testing the consumer of an interface publishes its expectations, the provider runs them in its own pipeline, and a break prompts a conversation before release; Pact is the example tool given (Vocke, 26 February 2018) ([Practical Test Pyramid](https://martinfowler.com/articles/practical-test-pyramid.html)).

### The testing trophy

**[DURABLE]** The testing trophy (Kent C. Dodds, 3 June 2021) has four layers, static, unit, integration and end-to-end, with most weight on integration tests because they give the most confidence per unit of effort. Its base layer is static checking with type checkers and linters, added because static checking is not a given in JavaScript, and the author limits the model to front-end JavaScript applications and monoliths, not microservices or serverless systems ([Testing Trophy](https://kentcdodds.com/blog/the-testing-trophy-and-testing-classifications)).

**[INFERENCE]** Choose the test shape by architecture: a trophy-like emphasis on integration and component tests for a front end or a modular monolith, and a pyramid plus contract tests where there are separately deployed services. Both sources agree on few end-to-end tests and on speed and trust mattering more than ratios, so the two models differ in emphasis and do not contradict each other.

### Mutation testing moved to Adopt in November 2026

**[FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 35 (November 2026) places mutation testing in Adopt, up from Trial in Volume 34 (April 2026), where it was described as a way to expose AI-generated tests that always pass ([Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques); [Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf)). Volume 35 also lists "tests as data over tests as code", meaning cases held as structured data and run by a project harness, in Assess ([Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)). **[INFERENCE]** When agents write tests, add a mutation-score gate on changed code to the definition of done, because coverage alone no longer separates real tests from decorative ones. **[GAP]** No sourced coverage or mutation-score threshold was retrieved, so the gate value must be set from the team's own baseline.

### Static analysis, type checking and agent feedback sensors

**[DURABLE]** DORA orders the pipeline with unit tests and static analysis first, then acceptance and non-functional tests such as performance and vulnerability scans, then manual exploration ([DORA test automation](https://dora.dev/capabilities/test-automation/)). **[FAST-MOVING, Apr 2026 and Nov 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 34 (April 2026) places feedback sensors for coding agents in Trial, meaning compilers, linters and tests are wired into the agent loop so failures trigger self-correction, and Volume 35 (November 2026) places threat modelling and agent sandboxing in Adopt as defaults ([Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf); [Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)). **[GAP]** No primary evidence was retrieved on the defect-reduction effect of static typing or of specific static-analysis tools.

### TypeScript's position

**[VENDOR]** GitHub's Octoverse 2026 (published 28 October 2026, covering 1 October 2025 to 30 September 2026) ranks TypeScript first by contributors, Python second and JavaScript third ([GitHub Octoverse 2026](https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/octoverse-commits-pushes-reach-new-highs-in-2026-as-developers-build-with-agents/)); the 2025 edition's headline was that TypeScript had become the most used language on GitHub ([GitHub Octoverse index](https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/)).

### Feature flags: four types with different lifetimes

**[DURABLE]** Pete Hodgson (2016, revised 9 October 2017) distinguishes four toggle types: release toggles (days to a couple of weeks, static), experiment toggles (hours to weeks, decided per user), ops toggles (mostly short-lived, with some permanent kill switches, and changeable without a deploy), and permission toggles (which can last years and are decided per request). Decision logic should sit behind one decision object, tests should cover the expected production configuration, the planned release configuration and the fallback instead of every combination, and the convention is that a flag switched off means the old behaviour ([Feature Toggles](https://martinfowler.com/articles/feature-toggles.html)).

### Feature flags: controlling toggle debt

**[DURABLE]** Hodgson's controls for toggle debt (revised 9 October 2017) are to create the removal task when the flag is created, give flags expiry dates, use "time bombs" that fail the build after expiry, and cap the number of live flags ([Feature Toggles](https://martinfowler.com/articles/feature-toggles.html)). **[GAP]** OpenFeature and vendor flag platforms were not researched, and no 2024 to 2026 primary source on feature-flag practice was retrieved.

### Error budgets from the application side

**[DURABLE]** Google's example error budget policy releases normally while the service is within its service-level objective. If the budget for the preceding four weeks is spent, all changes halt except P0 fixes and security fixes until the service is back within objective. A single incident that consumes more than 20% of the four-week budget requires a postmortem with at least one P0 action, and a class of outage consuming more than 20% over a quarter requires a P0 item in the next quarter's plan. Feature work may continue when the cause was external (a company-wide network problem, another team's service, or out-of-scope load), and disputes escalate to the CTO ([SRE Workbook error budget policy](https://sre.google/workbook/error-budget-policy/)). **[INFERENCE]** A team that is not Google can reuse the policy's shape with its own numbers: a written objective per user journey, a four-week window, a pre-agreed rule for what stops when the budget is gone, and the 20% postmortem trigger.

### Observability in code: OpenTelemetry status

**[FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026]** OpenTelemetry's specification status page shows the tracing API, SDK and protocol as stable with long-term support, the metrics API and protocol as stable with mixed SDK status, baggage as stable and the logs bridge API as stable ([OTel spec status](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/status/)). The language SDK table shows traces and metrics stable in Java, JavaScript, Python, Go and .NET; logs stable in Java and .NET, at release candidate in Go, and still in development in JavaScript and Python; and profiles in development for Java only ([OTel status](https://opentelemetry.io/status/index.md)). Blog titles on the site in 2026 record that OpenTelemetry graduated in the CNCF, that declarative configuration became stable and that Profiles entered public alpha; only the titles were read ([OTel status page](https://opentelemetry.io/status/)).

**[INFERENCE]** OpenTelemetry traces and metrics are safe to standardise on across the common stacks. For JavaScript and Python logs, keep a structured logging library with trace-ID correlation until the logs SDK is marked stable. **[GAP]** Semantic-convention stability for databases, messaging and generative AI was cut off in the pages fetched.

### Observability in code: correlation, log streams and agent-readable telemetry

**[DURABLE]** Applications should propagate a correlation ID header on every request and response ([Azure API design, updated 8 May 2025](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/best-practices/api-design)) and write logs as an event stream, leaving routing and storage to the environment ([12factor.net](https://12factor.net/)). **[FAST-MOVING, Apr 2026 and Nov 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 34 (April 2026) lists SigNoz (OpenTelemetry-native observability) and Langfuse (LLM observability) among Trial platforms, and Volume 35 (November 2026) lists production-aware coding agents, which can read production telemetry, in Assess ([Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf); [Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)).

## 5. CI/CD: one automated path, small first exposure, automatic rollback

### Pipeline shape: four phases and one pipeline per change type

**[VENDOR]** Amazon's deployment pipelines have four phases (source, build, test, production) and a separate pipeline for each change type (application code, infrastructure, operating-system patches, configuration and feature flags, operator tools), each with the same safety mechanisms including automatic rollback. Builds run without network access for reproducibility, artifacts are signed, and packages are immutable and checksummed so that a rollback restores a known-good artifact ([AWS Builders' Library](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)). The article's date was not captured, and the researchers believe it predates 2024, so its structure is durable and its timings are Amazon-specific.

### Pre-production stages

**[VENDOR]** Amazon's pre-production stages are alpha and beta, which run functional API and end-to-end tests, and gamma, which is as production-like as possible with the same configuration, alarms and canary tests; integration tests call real dependencies and include negative cases ([AWS Builders' Library](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)). **[DURABLE]** DORA wants all suites run on every pipeline trigger in the order unit and static analysis, then acceptance and non-functional, then manual exploration ([DORA test automation](https://dora.dev/capabilities/test-automation/)), and twelve-factor asks for strict separation of build, release and run with development, staging and production kept as similar as possible ([12factor.net](https://12factor.net/)).

### Path-to-production mapping and agent pipeline responsibility

**[FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 35 (November 2026) places path-to-production mapping in Adopt, meaning a workshop that traces the whole delivery path to find bottlenecks beyond coding speed, and places agent pipeline responsibility, where agents are held accountable for keeping the build green, in Assess ([Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)).

### Progressive delivery: Amazon's one-box, waves and bake times

**[VENDOR]** Amazon's rollout pattern starts with a one-box stage that takes at most about 10% of traffic with alarms scoped to that box, then proceeds in waves of growing size, one availability zone or cell at a time, with rolling batches replacing at most 33% of hosts. Rollback is automatic on a team-level aggregate alarm covering fault rate, latency percentiles, resource use and canary tests. Bake time is about 1 hour after the one-box stage, at least 12 hours after the first regional wave and 2 to 4 hours after later waves, and promotion requires a minimum number of data points ([AWS Builders' Library](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)). A typical Amazon pipeline takes about four to five business days to reach all regions because of bake times, and urgent fixes may shorten bake time only with senior review and manual monitoring ([AWS Builders' Library](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)).

**[INFERENCE]** Amazon's multi-day, multi-region bake schedule is sized for a hyperscale blast radius. A single-region EU SaaS can keep the structure (production-like stage, small first exposure, alarm-gated promotion, automatic rollback) and compress the timings to minutes or hours; no source prescribing bake times for small systems was found. **[GAP]** No AWS-native progressive-delivery documentation (CodeDeploy, ECS, Lambda traffic shifting) was fetched.

### Rollback rules and deployment windows

**[VENDOR]** Amazon triggers rollback automatically from alarms, scopes one-box alarms to that box, allows rollbacks to replace hosts faster than a roll-forward, and blocks new production deployments while the high-severity alarm is firing. Deployments are limited to business hours, avoiding nights, weekends, holidays, Fridays and late afternoons so that on-call engineers are available, with a warning that very narrow windows cause changes to pile up ([AWS Builders' Library](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)). **[FAST-MOVING, Sep 2025]** DORA's 2025 report identifies frequent use of rollback as the version-control behaviour that amplifies team performance under AI adoption, and "fortify safety nets" is one of its recommendations to leaders ([DORA 2025 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf); [Google Cloud announcement, 23 Sep 2025](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report)).

### Rollback during a schema change

**[DURABLE]** Each expand, migrate and contract phase is separately releasable, which is what makes application rollback safe during a schema change ([ParallelChange, 13 May 2014](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/ParallelChange.html)); migrations themselves are numbered, tracked per instance and run by the same automated process as code ([DORA database change management](https://dora.dev/capabilities/database-change-management/)).

DORA publishes distributions in 2025 and no longer publishes elite, high, medium and low cut-offs in the material extracted.

### CI health benchmarks from CircleCI telemetry

**[VENDOR]** CircleCI's 2026 State of Software Delivery (blog of 18 February 2026, based on 28,738,317 workflows run on its platform in September 2025 from projects with at least two contributors) reports that main-branch success rate fell to 70.8% and typical time to return to green was 72 minutes, 13% longer than a year earlier; CircleCI's own stated benchmarks are a 90% success rate and 60-minute recovery ([CircleCI, 18 Feb 2026](https://circleci.com/blog/five-takeaways-2026-software-delivery-report)). Average daily workflow runs rose 59% year on year, but the median team rose 4%, the bottom quartile not at all, and the top 5% rose 97% ([CircleCI, 18 Feb 2026](https://circleci.com/blog/five-takeaways-2026-software-delivery-report)).

**[VENDOR]** The same CircleCI data (September 2025 workflows, published 18 February 2026) shows that for the median team feature-branch throughput rose 15% while main-branch throughput fell 7%; for a top-10% team feature-branch activity rose almost 50% and main-branch throughput 1%; only the top 5% converted activity into delivery, with main-branch throughput up 26%. Companies of 21 to 50 employees had the lowest throughput and recovery times near three hours, almost four times those of the smallest (2 to 5) and largest (1,000 or more) cohorts ([CircleCI, 18 Feb 2026](https://circleci.com/blog/five-takeaways-2026-software-delivery-report)).

**[INFERENCE]** Code generation is no longer the constraint; review, pipeline reliability and merge are. Track main-branch success rate (target 90%), time to green (target under 60 minutes) and main-branch throughput, and do not track feature-branch activity or pull-request counts. CircleCI attributes the changes to AI without a controlled comparison, and its figures cover its own platform only.

### Development environments and preview deployments

**[FAST-MOVING, Apr 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 34 (April 2026) has Dev Containers in Trial for isolated, reproducible development environments and pixel-streamed development environments in Caution ([Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf)). **[GAP]** No primary source on preview or ephemeral environments was read in any research pass. **[INFERENCE]** Preview deployments per pull request fit the parity and fast-feedback principles, but only when each preview gets isolated data and no production secrets; this is inference and not evidence.

## 6. Engineering metrics: five DORA measures, and AI adds throughput along with instability

### The five DORA delivery metrics

**[DURABLE]** DORA now defines five software delivery metrics in two groups. Throughput covers change lead time (commit to production), deployment frequency and failed deployment recovery time. Instability covers change fail rate (deployments needing immediate intervention) and deployment rework rate (unplanned deployments caused by a production incident) ([DORA metrics guide](https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics/)). The history is that four metrics settled in 2015, availability was added in 2018 and broadened to reliability in 2021, mean time to restore was renamed failed deployment recovery time in 2023 and narrowed to change-caused failures, and rework rate was added in 2024 ([DORA metrics history](https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics/history/)).

### How to use DORA metrics and what to avoid

**[DURABLE]** DORA's guidance is to apply the metrics per application or service, to treat speed and stability as mutually reinforcing and not as a trade-off, and to share all five across development, operations and release roles. The pitfalls it names are using metrics as targets (Goodhart's law), relying on a single metric, comparing unlike systems, siloed ownership, competition between teams, and building measurement plumbing before improving anything; the suggested start is the DORA Quick Check plus a team conversation ([DORA metrics guide](https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics/)).

### Do not measure coding throughput

**[FAST-MOVING, Apr 2026 and Nov 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 34 (April 2026) lists DORA metrics, explicitly including rework rate, in Adopt as the alternative to counting AI-generated lines, and both Volume 34 and Volume 35 (November 2026) put coding throughput as a productivity measure (lines, pull-request counts, AI-code percentage, token counts) in Caution ([Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf); [Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)). **[FAST-MOVING, May 2026; secondary press report]** A press round-up reported that Amazon closed an internal token leaderboard after it was gamed and that Uber exhausted its 2026 AI budget in four months without a measurable productivity rise ([TechCrunch, 29 May 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/29/coders-are-refusing-to-work-without-ai-and-that-could-come-back-to-bite-them/)).

### DORA 2025: the headline and the sample

**[FAST-MOVING, Sep 2025]** DORA's 2025 report was published on 23 September 2025 from a survey fielded 13 June to 21 July 2025 with nearly 5,000 respondents and more than 100 hours of interviews, and it is the latest annual report as of November 2026. Its headline is that AI amplifies an organisation's existing strengths and weaknesses ([Google Cloud announcement, 23 Sep 2025](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report); [DORA research](https://dora.dev/research/); [DORA 2025 report page](https://dora.dev/research/2025/dora-report/)). The report is published by Google and rests on self-reported survey data.

### DORA 2025: seven team profiles

**[FAST-MOVING, Sep 2025]** DORA's 2025 report replaces the old performance tiers with seven team profiles: foundational challenges (10%), legacy bottleneck (11%), constrained by process (17%), high impact with low cadence (7%), stable and methodical (15%), pragmatic performers (20%) and harmonious high-achievers (20%) ([DORA 2025 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf)). **[CONTESTED]** One practitioner reading (1 October 2025) holds that in four of the clusters speed gains are largely cancelled by higher change-failure rates and that about 40% of respondents sit in the two clusters that gain without added instability; this is commentary and not a DORA statement ([Rob Bowley, 1 Oct 2025](https://blog.robbowley.net/2025/10/01/dora-2025-ai-assisted-dev-report-some-benefit-most-dont/)).

### DORA 2025: AI use, trust and perceived gains

**[FAST-MOVING, Sep 2025]** In DORA's 2025 survey 90% of respondents used AI at work (up 14 points), the median was two hours on the most recent workday, 65% relied on it heavily, over 80% believed it raised their productivity and 59% reported better code quality. Trust in AI output was "a lot" or "a great deal" for 24%, "somewhat" for 46% and little or none for 30% ([DORA 2025 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf); [Google blog on DORA 2025](https://blog.google/technology/developers/dora-report-2025/)).

### DORA 2025: measured associations of AI adoption

**[FAST-MOVING, Sep 2025]** DORA's 2025 report finds AI adoption positively associated with delivery throughput, product performance, code quality, individual effectiveness and team and organisational performance, and still associated with higher delivery instability, with no measurable relationship to burnout or friction. This reverses part of 2024, when each 25% rise in AI adoption was associated with roughly 1.5% lower throughput and 7.2% lower stability ([DORA 2025 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf)).

### DORA AI Capabilities Model: seven amplifiers

**[FAST-MOVING, Sep 2025]** The DORA AI Capabilities Model names seven capabilities that amplify the benefit of AI: a clear and communicated AI stance, healthy data ecosystems, AI-accessible internal data, strong version control practices, working in small batches, a user-centric focus (without which AI adoption harms team performance) and a quality internal platform ([DORA 2025 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf)); the companion guide was last updated 25 November 2025 ([DORA AI capabilities model](https://dora.dev/ai/capabilities-model/)). On the AI stance, what DORA measures is the clarity and awareness of the policy and not its content ([DORA 2025 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf)).

**[INFERENCE]** For a department of AI agents the seven capabilities translate directly into a written AI usage policy, agents connected to internal documentation and code context, small work items, frequent commits with practised rollback, a golden-path platform, and a user-outcome check on every piece of work.

### DORA 2025: value stream management and platform quality

**[FAST-MOVING, Sep 2025]** DORA's 2025 report finds that teams which regularly review their value stream report higher team and product performance and that value stream management strongly moderates AI's effect on organisational performance; its starter test is whether the team can draw its delivery value stream on a whiteboard. AI's effect on organisational performance is negligible where platform quality is low and strong where it is high ([DORA 2025 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf)). **[GAP]** DORA also lists a report on the return on investment of AI-assisted development that covers an initial productivity dip, but no figures were retrieved ([DORA AI page](https://dora.dev/ai/)).

### SPACE: measure several dimensions, including one perceptual measure

**[DURABLE]** The SPACE framework (Forsgren, Storey, Maddila, Zimmermann, Houck and Butler, ACM Queue 2021) has five dimensions: satisfaction and well-being, performance, activity, communication and collaboration, and efficiency and flow. Its rules are to measure at least three dimensions, include at least one perceptual (survey) measure and avoid taking two metrics from one dimension, and it rejects the myths that productivity equals activity, that one metric suffices and that productivity is only about individuals. It lists onboarding time for new members as a collaboration metric ([ACM Queue SPACE](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3454124)).

### DevEx: feedback loops, cognitive load, flow state

**[DURABLE]** The DevEx framework (Storey, Forsgren and Greiler, ACM Queue 2023) has three dimensions: feedback loops, cognitive load and flow state. It combines perceptual measures with workflow measures (build time, review turnaround, deployment lead time, meeting load), recommends surveying quarterly or semi-annually, breaking results down by team and persona, and acting on results to avoid survey fatigue ([ACM Queue DevEx](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3595878)).

**[INFERENCE]** A minimal metrics set consistent with SPACE and DevEx is the five DORA metrics per service, one quarterly developer-experience survey, and two workflow measures (continuous-integration feedback time and review turnaround), reviewed as trends and never used to rank teams.

### Missing context is the commonest developer barrier

**[FAST-MOVING, 2026]** Stack Overflow's 2026 Developer Survey (30,903 responses from 169 countries) reports that 63% cite incomplete information as a barrier, 79% regularly discover important context only after starting or finishing a task, and 75% spend five or more hours searching for answers (the period is not stated in the extract); job mood was 22% happy, 45% complacent and 33% not happy ([Stack Overflow 2026 survey](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2026/)).

### Controlled evidence on AI speed-up: one slowdown, one unreliable follow-up

**[FAST-MOVING, Feb 2026]** METR's randomised trial in early 2025 with experienced open-source developers found tasks took about 19% longer when AI was allowed (95% confidence interval from 2% to 39% longer), while participants believed they had been sped up ([METR update, 24 Feb 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)). METR's follow-up from August 2025 (57 developers, 143 repositories, more than 800 tasks) estimated an 18% speed-up for returning developers (interval from 38% faster to 9% slower) and 4% for new ones (interval from 15% faster to 9% slower); both intervals include zero ([METR update, 24 Feb 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)).

**[FAST-MOVING, Feb 2026]** METR itself says the follow-up data is too compromised to rely on: developers declined to work without AI, 30 to 50% withheld tasks they did not want to do unaided, pay dropped from $150 to $50 an hour, and timing breaks down when people run several agents at once. METR believes developers are probably faster in early 2026 than in early 2025, regards its estimate as a likely lower bound, calls the evidence very weak and is redesigning the study ([METR update, 24 Feb 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)). A practitioner commentary stresses that the 18% figure is not a finding METR stands behind and that these studies cover solo open-source tasks and not team delivery ([Rob Bowley blog, 4 Apr 2026](https://blog.robbowley.net/2026/04/04/metrs-developer-productivity-research-2026-update/)).

### Code-quality signals under AI assistance

**[VENDOR]** GitClear's analysis of 211 million changed lines from 2020 to 2024 found cloned or copy-pasted lines rose from 8.3% to 12.3%, refactoring-type "moved" lines fell from 25% in 2021 to under 10% in 2024, and copy and paste exceeded moved code for the first time; the data ends in 2024 and is ageing ([GitClear 2025 research](https://www.gitclear.com/ai_assistant_code_quality_2025_research)). **[VENDOR; secondary press report, May 2026]** CodeRabbit, a vendor of AI review tools, reported that AI pull requests carried 1.7 times more issues than human ones, and a Singapore Management University paper (arXiv 2603.28592) warns of long-term maintenance cost; both were relayed by a press round-up and not read at source ([TechCrunch, 29 May 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/29/coders-are-refusing-to-work-without-ai-and-that-could-come-back-to-bite-them/)).

### Radar positions on coding-agent practice

**[FAST-MOVING, Apr 2026 and Nov 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar in 2026 has Claude Code and Cursor in Adopt (Volume 34, April 2026); agent sandboxing in Adopt; open-weight models, subagents and unattended overnight agentic builds in Trial; and codebase cognitive debt, agent skill cognitive surrender and blanket frontier model usage in Caution (Volumes 34 and 35) ([Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf); [Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)).

### What the evidence adds up to for planning

**[INFERENCE]** Across DORA, METR, Stack Overflow, JetBrains, GitHub, CircleCI and Thoughtworks the consistent picture is near-universal AI use, strong perceived gains, a positive association with organisational throughput, a persistent instability cost, and no trustworthy measurement of individual speed-up. A manager agent should plan capacity on measured delivery outcomes (the five DORA metrics, especially change fail rate and rework rate, plus main-branch success rate) and never on lines, pull requests or tokens produced.

## 7. Maintenance: debt, dependencies, documentation, on-call, postmortems and planning

### Technical debt: the quadrant and when not to repay

**[DURABLE]** Fowler's technical debt quadrant (2009) classifies debt as reckless or prudent and as deliberate or inadvertent. Prudent inadvertent debt is inevitable as teams learn, reckless debt should be avoided, and interest should be weighed against principal, so rarely touched code may not be worth repaying ([Technical Debt Quadrant](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/TechnicalDebtQuadrant.html)). **[GAP]** No quantified technical-debt benchmarks (share of time spent on debt, cost studies) were retrieved.

### Cognitive debt in agent-built systems

**[FAST-MOVING, Apr 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 34 (April 2026) names codebase cognitive debt as a new form of debt, in which teams lose understanding of AI-generated systems and can no longer change them safely ([Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf)). **[INFERENCE]** For an agent-run department this is the debt to track: require a human-readable ADR or summary for every agent-built subsystem and prune generated documentation.

### Legacy and process drag in DORA's profiles

**[FAST-MOVING, Sep 2025]** DORA's 2025 profiles "legacy bottleneck" (11% of respondents) and "constrained by process" (17%) describe teams where unstable systems or inefficient process drive reactive work and burnout ([DORA 2025 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf)). DORA also warns that heavier process added in response to stability problems lengthens lead time and enlarges batches in a worsening cycle ([DORA streamlining change approval](https://dora.dev/capabilities/streamlining-change-approval/)).

### Dependency updates: Renovate's practice set

**[VENDOR]** Renovate's upgrade guidance (Mend) is to update often so each update is small, take major versions one at a time and in good time, automerge development tools when checks pass and optionally patch releases, and set `minimumReleaseAge` to 14 days for automerged third-party updates so registries have time to catch malicious packages. It also advises pinning container images to digests and third-party GitHub Actions to full commit SHAs, running weekly lockfile maintenance, routing major updates through the dependency dashboard with changelog review by more than one person, prioritising critical CVE fixes with vulnerability alerts enabled, and pinning development tool versions so the whole team runs identical linters and formatters ([Renovate upgrade best practices](https://docs.renovatebot.com/upgrade-best-practices/)). The documentation page was read in November 2026.

**[INFERENCE]** A 14-day release-age delay conflicts with fast patching of actively exploited vulnerabilities; handle this by exempting security advisories from the delay, which Renovate's advice to prioritise CVE fixes supports. **[CONTESTED]** The cool-down length is a judgement call inside the research: Renovate documents 14 days, while one researcher's unsourced judgement was 3 to 7 days on the grounds that the 2025 to 2026 worms were removed within hours to days. The sourced 14-day figure is the default here.

### Post-quantum cryptography as a maintenance item

**[FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 35 (November 2026) places post-quantum cryptography in Adopt and names key exchange as the most urgent migration ([Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)).

### Documentation quality multiplies technical practices

**[DURABLE]** DORA's documentation research (2021 to 2022 data) links documentation quality to organisational performance and finds it amplifies every technical capability studied. The reported lift with above-average against below-average documentation is 1,525% against 36% for trunk-based development, 750% against 34% for continuous integration and 656% against 63% for continuous delivery. DORA measures documentation through survey statements on attributes such as clarity, findability and reliability ([DORA documentation quality](https://dora.dev/capabilities/documentation-quality/)).

**[FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 35 (November 2026) places agent-maintained knowledge bases that are plain-text and human-curated in Trial and AI-driven documentation sprawl in Caution ([Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)).

### On-call load limits from Google SRE

**[DURABLE]** Google's SRE book (2016) sets on-call limits of at most 50% of time on operational work and at most 25% on call, no more than two incidents per 12-hour shift, and a median of zero pages a day as sustainable with more than one as unsustainable. It asks for a minimum rotation of eight engineers at one site or six per site across two sites, response in about 5 minutes for user-facing critical services and about 30 minutes for less sensitive ones, and compensation for out-of-hours work with time off or pay ([SRE Book: Being On-Call](https://sre.google/sre-book/being-on-call/)). **[INFERENCE]** A small team cannot meet the eight-person rotation; the usable parts are the load limits and the compensation principle, with the rotation pooled across teams.

### On-call in Sweden: working-time rules that shape rotations

**[DURABLE; checked on primary source]** The Swedish Working Hours Act, Arbetstidslag (1982:673), caps ordinary working time at 40 hours a week (5 §) and total working time at an average of 48 hours per seven-day period over a reference period of at most four months (10 b §). Jourtid, meaning time at the workplace at the employer's disposal, is capped at 48 hours per four weeks or 50 hours per calendar month, and time actually worked is not jourtid (6 §). General overtime is capped at 48 hours per four weeks or 50 per month and 200 hours per calendar year (8 §), with extra overtime of a further 150 hours per year (8 a §), both staying inside the four-week and monthly caps ([SFS 1982:673](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/sfs-1982-673.html)).

**[DURABLE; checked on primary source]** Arbetstidslag (1982:673) requires at least 11 consecutive hours of daily rest in every 24-hour period, including midnight to 05:00 unless the nature of the work requires otherwise, with temporary deviation only for unforeseeable circumstances and with equivalent compensatory rest (13 §). Weekly rest is at least 36 consecutive hours in every seven-day period, and standby time (beredskap), when the employee may be away from the workplace but must be available, does not count as weekly rest (14 §). Night is 22:00 to 06:00 and night workers are limited to eight hours per 24 on average over four months (13 a §) ([SFS 1982:673](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/sfs-1982-673.html)).

**[DURABLE; checked on primary source]** Under Arbetstidslag (1982:673) a collective agreement concluded or approved by a central employee organisation may replace the act entirely or deviate from most of these sections within the floor of the EU Working Time Directive (3 §), and Arbetsmiljöverket supervises and can impose sanction fees for breaches made without collective-agreement support (20, 26 and 27 §§) ([SFS 1982:673](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/sfs-1982-673.html)).

**[INFERENCE; legal orientation only]** Absent a collective agreement, three design consequences follow for developer on-call in Sweden. A week of unbroken standby leaves no qualifying weekly rest, so rotations need a 36-hour standby-free block every seven days. A night call-out interrupts the 11-hour daily rest, which is tolerable only as a temporary, unforeseen deviation with compensatory rest, so routine night pages argue for follow-the-sun cover or a late start the next day. Hours actually worked on a call-out count toward the overtime caps and the 48-hour average. **[GAP]** Most Swedish technology employers under collective agreements follow the agreement's own on-call rules, which were not researched (for example the Almega IT agreement), and the act's exemption for managerial or self-regulated positions (2 §) was not checked. AI agents carrying first-line response do not remove these limits for the humans who are paged.

### Incident postmortems

**[DURABLE]** Google's SRE book asks teams to define postmortem triggers in advance: user-visible downtime or degradation past a threshold, any data loss, on-call intervention such as a rollback, resolution time past a threshold, or a monitoring failure. A postmortem contains impact, mitigation actions, root causes and follow-ups, is blameless (assume good intent and fix systems, not people), and is reviewed, stored where others can find it and shared widely without data identifying end users ([SRE Book: Postmortem Culture](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/)). The quantified trigger from the SRE workbook is any single incident using more than 20% of the four-week error budget, which needs a postmortem with at least one P0 action ([SRE Workbook error budget policy](https://sre.google/workbook/error-budget-policy/)).

### Planning and estimation: Shape Up as one documented option

**[VENDOR]** Basecamp's Shape Up uses six-week cycles followed by a two-week cool-down, sets an "appetite" (how much time an idea is worth) instead of estimating, has senior people shape work before it is bet on, chooses work at a betting table during cool-down, applies a circuit breaker so that work not shipped in one cycle stops by default, and shows progress from unknown to known on hill charts ([Shape Up, chapter 1](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/0.3-chapter-01)). It is a single-company account from 2019.

**[FAST-MOVING, Sep 2025]** DORA's 2025 report finds that working in small batches improves product performance and reduces friction under AI adoption and that regular value-stream review raises team performance ([DORA 2025 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf)).

**[GAP]** No empirical study comparing Scrum, Kanban and Shape Up, and no study of estimation accuracy (story points, forecasting from throughput, #NoEstimates, Jørgensen's expert-estimation research), was opened in any research pass; the Scrum Guide and Kanban Guide were not fetched. **[INFERENCE]** The only planning practice with cross-study support in the sources is small batch size with short feedback loops. Scrum, Kanban and Shape Up can each deliver that, so present them as options chosen by fit (interrupt-driven work suits flow-based Kanban; fixed-appetite project work suits Shape Up), judge by lead time and rework rate instead of framework fidelity, and say plainly that the choice is practitioner judgement.

## 8. Team practices: the platform is the golden path and standards live in tools

### What an internal platform is

**[DURABLE]** The CNCF Platforms white paper defines a platform as an integrated set of capabilities presented according to its users' needs. Its attributes are being run as a product, consistent interfaces, golden paths (templates plus documentation for build, scan, test, deploy and observe), self-service, lower cognitive load, being optional and composable, and being secure by default ([CNCF Platforms white paper](https://tag-app-delivery.cncf.io/whitepapers/platforms/)). The page shows version 1.0 without a date; the researchers believe it is from 2023, unverified.

### Platform capability areas and how to measure a platform

**[DURABLE]** The CNCF white paper lists thirteen capability areas, including portals, provisioning APIs and command-line tools, golden-path templates, build and test automation, delivery and verification, development environments, observability, infrastructure, data services, messaging, identity and secrets, security, and artifact storage. It measures a platform by active users and retention, satisfaction surveys, time from request to fulfilment, time to build and deploy a new service, time for a new user to submit a first code change, and the DORA metrics of its users ([CNCF Platforms white paper](https://tag-app-delivery.cncf.io/whitepapers/platforms/)).

### Platform quality findings from DORA 2025

**[FAST-MOVING, Sep 2025]** DORA's 2025 report finds 90% of organisations have at least one internal platform and 76% a dedicated platform team; quality platforms go with higher organisational and product performance, productivity and well-being; reliability and security are rated well while user-experience features such as acting on feedback and task automation lag; and heavy AI users on high-quality platforms reported more friction, which DORA suggests is the guardrails working ([DORA 2025 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf)).

### Golden paths for agents: reference applications and shared skills

**[FAST-MOVING, Apr 2026 and Nov 2026]** Thoughtworks Radar Volume 35 (November 2026) puts anchoring coding agents to a maintained reference application, in place of static templates, in Trial, along with agent plugin marketplaces for sharing skills across an organisation, and puts an "enterprise harness" of shared skills, tools and policies in Assess; Volume 34 (April 2026) has Port as an internal developer portal in Trial ([Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques); [Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf)). **[INFERENCE]** For a department of AI worker agents the golden path is the harness itself: service templates, a reference application, a short curated instruction file, sandboxed execution, and pipeline checks that enforce standards. The same artefacts serve human onboarding.

### Coding standards: the style guide decides and tools enforce

**[DURABLE]** Google's review standard makes the style guide the final authority on style; anything the guide does not cover is preference, where the reviewer follows existing code or defers to the author. Design choices are judged on engineering principle, and data beats opinion ([Google review standard](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/standard.html)). **[FAST-MOVING, Apr 2026 and Nov 2026]** The 2026 Radar volumes advise keeping shared agent instruction files curated and minimal and converting rules into automated checks ([Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf); [Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)). **[INFERENCE]** Standards that can be automated (formatting, linting, types, dependency pins, accessibility checks) should never be review comments; human review is reserved for design and risk.

### Onboarding humans and agents

**[DURABLE]** Three sourced practices speed onboarding. Pairing passes project knowledge through the partner, and strong-style pairing suits knowledge transfer ([On Pair Programming, 15 Jan 2020](https://martinfowler.com/articles/on-pair-programming.html)). Code review is a teaching channel for language, framework and design knowledge, with non-essential teaching comments labelled as optional ([Google review standard](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/standard.html)). Reproducible environments such as Dev Containers, in Trial in Radar Volume 34 (April 2026), remove set-up variance ([Radar Vol 34 PDF](https://www.thoughtworks.com/content/dam/thoughtworks/documents/radar/2026/04/tr_technology_radar_vol_34_en.pdf)).

**[INFERENCE]** Track time to first merged change for each new human or agent worker as the single onboarding metric, since both SPACE and the CNCF white paper point at it ([ACM Queue SPACE](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3454124); [CNCF Platforms white paper](https://tag-app-delivery.cncf.io/whitepapers/platforms/)). **[GAP]** No quantitative research on onboarding-time benchmarks or onboarding-programme effectiveness was retrieved, and Team Topologies and the CNCF platform maturity model were not fetched.

## 9. Application security: the 2025 OWASP lists, verified authentication rules and threat modelling

### OWASP Top 10:2025 categories

**[FAST-MOVING, 2025 edition]** The OWASP Top 10:2025 is the current edition and lists A01 Broken Access Control, A02 Security Misconfiguration, A03 Software Supply Chain Failures, A04 Cryptographic Failures, A05 Injection, A06 Insecure Design, A07 Authentication Failures, A08 Software or Data Integrity Failures, A09 Security Logging and Alerting Failures, and A10 Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions ([OWASP Top 10:2025 Introduction](https://top10.owasp.org/2025/0x00_2025-Introduction/)). A secondary source dates the announcement to November 2025 and finalisation to January 2026; the OWASP page itself gave no date ([Patrowl, 29 June 2026](https://patrowl.io/en/blog/owasp-top-10-2025-what-s-changed-and-the-2026-data)).

### OWASP Top 10:2025: what changed from 2021 and the data behind it

**[FAST-MOVING, 2025 edition]** Compared with 2021, the 2025 list folds server-side request forgery into A01, raises Security Misconfiguration from fifth to second, widens the old vulnerable-components category into A03 covering dependencies, build systems and distribution infrastructure, drops Injection from third to fifth, adds A10 for improper error handling, logic errors and fail-open behaviour, and renames A09 to stress alerting. The data comes from contributors covering more than 2.8 million applications, with 589 CWEs in the dataset and 248 mapped to the ten categories; A03 and A09 were promoted by community survey and not by test data ([OWASP Top 10:2025 Introduction](https://top10.owasp.org/2025/0x00_2025-Introduction/)). **[INFERENCE]** A10 is a natural review focus for agent-written code, because agents often add broad exception handlers to make tests pass.

### OWASP API Security Top 10 (2023 edition)

**[FAST-MOVING, 2023 edition]** The OWASP API Security Top 10 site lists only the 2023 and 2019 editions, so 2023 is current. Its ten risks are API1 Broken Object Level Authorization (check permission on every access that takes an object ID), API2 Broken Authentication, API3 Broken Object Property Level Authorization (field-level read and write checks), API4 Unrestricted Resource Consumption (limits on compute, bandwidth and paid downstream calls), API5 Broken Function Level Authorization (admin against user functions), API6 Unrestricted Access to Sensitive Business Flows (automated abuse of a legitimate flow), API7 Server Side Request Forgery, API8 Security Misconfiguration, API9 Improper Inventory Management (undocumented, deprecated or debug endpoints and hosts) and API10 Unsafe Consumption of APIs (trusting third-party API data) ([OWASP API Security Top 10 2023](https://api-security.owasp.org/editions/2023/en/0x11-t10/)).

### OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 as a verification and procurement baseline

**[FAST-MOVING, May 2025]** OWASP's Application Security Verification Standard is at stable version 5.0.0, released 30 May 2025. OWASP positions it as a metric of trust in a web application, as guidance for developers of security controls, as a procurement requirement that can be written into contracts, and as a test basis; requirements are cited in the form `v<version>-<chapter>.<section>.<requirement>`, for example `v5.0.0-1.2.5`, and machine-readable CSV and JSON lists are on the project's GitHub ([OWASP ASVS](https://asvs.owasp.org/)). **[INFERENCE]** Citing ASVS requirement IDs in penetration-test scopes and customer answers gives a version-stable way to say what was verified. **[GAP]** The ASVS 5.0 level definitions, chapter count and requirement count are not on the landing page; the researchers' recollection of about 350 requirements in 17 chapters at three levels is unverified.

### OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025 edition)

**[FAST-MOVING, 2025 edition]** The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications shows 2025 as its current list: LLM01 Prompt Injection, LLM02 Sensitive Information Disclosure, LLM03 Supply Chain, LLM04 Data and Model Poisoning, LLM05 Improper Output Handling, LLM06 Excessive Agency, LLM07 System Prompt Leakage, LLM08 Vector and Embedding Weaknesses, LLM09 Misinformation and LLM10 Unbounded Consumption ([OWASP GenAI LLM Top 10](https://genai.owasp.org/llm-top-10/)).

### OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications for 2026

**[FAST-MOVING, Dec 2025]** OWASP published its Top 10 for Agentic Applications for 2026 on 9 December 2025 after peer review by more than 100 experts, aimed at agents that plan, act and decide across workflows ([OWASP GenAI resource page](https://genai.owasp.org/resource/owasp-top-10-for-agentic-applications-for-2026/)). **[VENDOR]** The ten risk names were read from a vendor summary and not from the OWASP PDF: ASI01 Agent Goal Hijack, ASI02 Tool Misuse and Exploitation, ASI03 Identity and Privilege Abuse, ASI04 Agentic Supply Chain Vulnerabilities, ASI05 Unexpected Code Execution, ASI06 Memory and Context Poisoning, ASI07 Insecure Inter-Agent Communication, ASI08 Cascading Failures, ASI09 Human-Agent Trust Exploitation and ASI10 Rogue Agents ([Cycode](https://cycode.com/blog/owasp-top-10-agentic-applications/)).

**[VENDOR]** The mitigation themes in that vendor summary of the OWASP agentic list are to separate instructions from retrieved content, require human confirmation for sensitive actions, give each agent its own identity with short-lived task-scoped credentials, use signed components and an AI bill of materials, run least-privilege sandboxes with default-deny egress, keep memory scoped or ephemeral, use mutual authentication between agents, add circuit breakers and separate development from production, show raw actions at approval time, and give every agent an owner, an expiry and a tested kill switch. "Least agency" means granting only the autonomy, tools and permissions the task needs ([Cycode](https://cycode.com/blog/owasp-top-10-agentic-applications/)).

### OAuth: 2.1 is still a draft, so cite the 2.0 best current practice

**[FAST-MOVING, Oct 2026]** OAuth 2.1 is not yet an RFC. The latest text is draft-ietf-oauth-v2-1-17 dated 31 October 2026, an active Internet-Draft with a working-group milestone of submission to the IESG in December 2026. Its confirmed rules are that PKCE is required, the implicit grant and the resource-owner password grant are removed, redirect URIs are compared by strict string match (the loopback port may vary), and sender-constrained tokens (DPoP or mutual TLS) are recommended ([IETF datatracker](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-oauth-v2-1/)). **[INFERENCE]** Because OAuth 2.1 is still a draft, design documents should say "OAuth 2.0 per the RFC 9700 best current practice with the OAuth 2.1 profile" and not "OAuth 2.1 compliant".

### Scope and session design rules

**[DURABLE]** The Model Context Protocol security guidance (specification revision 2025-06-18) gives scope rules usable for any API: start with a minimal low-risk scope set, elevate incrementally, never publish wildcard or omnibus scopes, log elevation events with correlation IDs, and authorise on the server instead of trusting claimed scopes. Its session rules are never to use session IDs as authentication, to generate non-deterministic IDs with a secure random generator, and to bind session IDs to the user ID on the server; it points implementers to RFC 9700 and OAuth 2.1 with HTTPS for all OAuth URLs except loopback ([MCP Security Best Practices](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/basic/security_best_practices)).

### Passwords under NIST SP 800-63B-4

**[FAST-MOVING, Aug 2025]** NIST SP 800-63B-4 (page generated 26 August 2025, superseding the previous SP 800-63B) requires at least 15 characters when a password is the only factor and at least 8 when it is used only as part of multi-factor authentication, with at least 64 characters allowed and spaces and Unicode accepted. It rules out composition rules, periodic forced changes (change only on evidence of compromise), security questions and hints; it requires checking new passwords against a blocklist of breached, common and context-specific values, allowing paste and password managers, salted hashing, and a cap of 100 consecutive failed attempts per authenticator ([NIST SP 800-63B-4](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html)). NIST binds US federal systems only and is a reference standard for a Swedish company.

### Passkeys and assurance levels under NIST SP 800-63B-4

**[FAST-MOVING, Aug 2025]** NIST SP 800-63B-4 (page generated 26 August 2025) allows syncable authenticators, its term for synced passkeys, at AAL1 and AAL2 and prohibits them at AAL3, which requires non-exportable keys. AAL2 needs two distinct factors or one multi-factor authenticator and the verifier must offer at least one phishing-resistant option; AAL3 requires phishing resistance and public-key proof of possession. Passwords, one-time passcodes, look-up secrets and out-of-band methods are not phishing-resistant ([NIST SP 800-63B-4](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html)).

### Session lifetimes under NIST SP 800-63B-4

**[FAST-MOVING, Aug 2025]** NIST SP 800-63B-4 (page generated 26 August 2025) recommends an overall session timeout of at most 30 days at AAL1 and at most 24 hours overall with 1 hour of inactivity at AAL2, and requires at most 12 hours overall at AAL3 with 15 minutes of inactivity recommended ([NIST SP 800-63B-4](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html)).

**[INFERENCE]** A defensible authentication baseline for a B2B SaaS drawn from SP 800-63B-4 is passkeys offered to every user, mandatory multi-factor authentication for admin and staff accounts, a 15-character minimum wherever password-only login remains, a breached-password blocklist, no rotation policy, and a 24-hour maximum session with a 1-hour idle timeout for sensitive areas.

### Server-side request forgery and URL handling

**[DURABLE]** The MCP security guidance (revision 2025-06-18) gives a server-side request forgery checklist that applies to any service fetching URLs: require HTTPS; block private ranges (10/8, 172.16/12, 192.168/16, 127/8, 169.254/16 including cloud metadata, fc00::/7 and fe80::/10); do not hand-roll IP parsing; validate every redirect hop; route through an egress proxy; and pin DNS between check and use. For URL handling it allows only http and https, rejects javascript:, data: and file: schemes, and forbids opening URLs through a shell ([MCP Security Best Practices](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/basic/security_best_practices)).

### Threat modelling: the four questions

**[DURABLE]** The Threat Modeling Manifesto frames threat modelling around four questions: what are we working on, what can go wrong, what are we going to do about it, and did we do a good enough job. Its values include finding and fixing design issues over checkbox compliance, people and collaboration over tools, and continuous refinement over a single delivery; its principles are to model early and often, fit the team's development practice, iterate over manageable parts of the system and judge by value to stakeholders ([Threat Modeling Manifesto](https://www.threatmodelingmanifesto.org/)).

### Threat modelling: anti-patterns

**[DURABLE]** The Threat Modeling Manifesto names four anti-patterns: the hero threat modeller (threat modelling is not a specialist-only skill), admiring the problem without producing fixes, over-focusing on one aspect, and assuming a single perfect diagram exists. Its positive patterns are a systematic approach, varied cross-functional viewpoints and field-tested techniques ([Threat Modeling Manifesto](https://www.threatmodelingmanifesto.org/)).

### Threat modelling: the four steps and STRIDE

**[DURABLE]** OWASP's Threat Modeling Cheat Sheet maps the four questions to four steps: model the system, usually with data-flow diagrams showing trust boundaries, data stores, processes and external entities; identify and rank threats by likelihood and impact; choose a response per threat and turn mitigations into security requirements; and review and validate with stakeholders. STRIDE pairs each threat with the property it violates: spoofing (authentication), tampering (integrity), repudiation (accountability), information disclosure (confidentiality), denial of service (availability) and elevation of privilege (authorisation) ([OWASP Threat Modeling Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Threat_Modeling_Cheat_Sheet.html)).

### Threat modelling: responses and validation

**[DURABLE]** OWASP's cheat sheet gives four responses to a threat: mitigate, eliminate (remove the feature), transfer (document the residual risk and its owner) and accept. Its validation checklist is that the diagram matches the system, every threat has an agreed response, mitigations are testable, and the model is documented with restricted access; the model must be maintained as the system changes ([OWASP Threat Modeling Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Threat_Modeling_Cheat_Sheet.html)). Thoughtworks Radar Volume 35 (November 2026) lists threat modelling in Adopt as a default ([Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)).

**[INFERENCE]** A lightweight routine for a product team is a 60-minute session per new feature or architecture change, one data-flow diagram, STRIDE applied at each trust-boundary crossing, each threat logged as a ticket with one of the four responses, and the model stored next to the design document. The steps are OWASP's; the time-box is the researchers'.

### NIST SSDF as the secure-development skeleton

**[DURABLE]** NIST SP 800-218, the Secure Software Development Framework version 1.1, was published in February 2022 and superseded a 2020 white paper ([NIST CSRC SP 800-218](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/218/final)). It groups practices into Prepare the Organization (PO), Protect the Software (PS), Produce Well-Secured Software (PW) and Respond to Vulnerabilities (RV); named examples on the project page are PO.5 secure development environments, PO.1.2 documenting security requirements for in-house software, PS.3.2 collecting and sharing provenance data for components, and PW.1.2 tracking security requirements, risks and design decisions. The project page, updated 6 October 2026, still presents version 1.1 as final ([NIST SSDF project page](https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/ssdf)). SP 800-218A is the finalised community profile for generative AI and dual-use foundation models and matters only if the company trains or fine-tunes models ([NIST SSDF project page](https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/ssdf)). **[GAP]** The researchers recall a draft version 1.2 from late 2025, but the project page does not mention it; its status is unverified.

### Security testing in the pipeline

**[PLATFORM CLAIM]** GitHub's cloud coding agent runs three checks on its own output before handing over, CodeQL static analysis, secret scanning and dependency analysis against known-vulnerable packages, and tries to fix what it finds ([GitHub Docs: Copilot coding agent](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/responsible-use/copilot-coding-agent)). Claude Code ships a `/security-review` command and a security-guidance plugin that reviews the agent's own changes ([Claude Code security docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/security)). Both pages were read in November 2026.

**[INFERENCE]** A pull-request gate set for an agent-heavy team, assembled from the sources in this document, is static analysis (with cross-site scripting and log-injection rules blocking, see section 14), secret scanning with push protection, software composition analysis plus a lockfile check, an infrastructure-as-code scan, a licence scan, unit and integration tests, and then human review. Dynamic testing and API fuzzing belong on a deployed preview or staging environment, nightly or before release. No single source prescribes this exact set.

### Vulnerability prioritisation and patch deadlines

**[DURABLE]** CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue lists a CVE only when there is reliable evidence of active exploitation and a clear remediation action. US federal civilian agencies must remediate within set timeframes under Binding Operational Directive 22-01, and CISA recommends that every organisation write a requirement to address catalogue entries immediately into its vulnerability-management plan and use the catalogue as an input to prioritisation frameworks such as SSVC ([CISA KEV catalogue](https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities)). **[FAST-MOVING, June 2026; secondary source]** A secondary source notes that the Trivy flaw CVE-2026-33634 was added to the catalogue only after exploitation, which illustrates why exploitation evidence matters more than CVSS alone ([Patrowl, 29 June 2026](https://patrowl.io/en/blog/owasp-top-10-2025-what-s-changed-and-the-2026-data)).

**[INFERENCE]** A workable internal patch policy anchored on those sources is to fix catalogue-listed or actively exploited vulnerabilities immediately, meaning within days, and other critical vulnerabilities within about 30 days. The 30-day figure is a convention and was not read at source, and no source read in the research sets deadlines for high or medium severities; confirm every tier against customer contracts and any ISO 27001 or SOC 2 commitments. **[GAP]** The exact deadlines in Binding Operational Directive 22-01 could not be opened (unverified recollection: two weeks for newly added entries), and PCI DSS patch requirements were not read.

## 10. Software supply chain: the registry and the pipeline are now the attack surface

### Supply chain is OWASP category A03

**[FAST-MOVING, 2025 edition]** OWASP made Software Supply Chain Failures category A03 in its 2025 Top 10, spanning dependencies, build systems and distribution infrastructure ([OWASP Top 10:2025](https://top10.owasp.org/2025/0x00_2025-Introduction/)).

### Shai-Hulud (September 2025) and npm's response

**[PLATFORM CLAIM]** GitHub was notified on 14 September 2025 of a self-replicating worm, Shai-Hulud, spreading through compromised npm maintainer accounts via malicious post-install scripts, and removed more than 500 compromised packages ([GitHub blog, 22 Sep 2025](https://github.blog/security/supply-chain-security/our-plan-for-a-more-secure-npm-supply-chain/)). **[VENDOR]** A security vendor counts about 700 malicious package versions; the figures differ because one counts packages and the other versions ([Xygeni](https://xygeni.io/?p=79254)).

**[PLATFORM CLAIM]** npm's announced response (22 September 2025) limits publishing to local publish with mandatory two-factor authentication, granular tokens with a seven-day lifetime, or trusted publishing; it deprecates classic tokens, plans to replace time-based one-time-password two-factor with FIDO-based two-factor, and removes the two-factor bypass for local publish. Trusted publishing through OIDC had become generally available in July 2025 ([GitHub blog, 22 Sep 2025](https://github.blog/security/supply-chain-security/our-plan-for-a-more-secure-npm-supply-chain/)). **[GAP]** The December 2025 npm changelog confirming classic-token revocation and token lifetime caps could not be opened, so exact current token limits are unverified.

### Shai-Hulud 2.0, GlassWorm and s1ngularity (late 2025)

**[VENDOR]** A vendor write-up describes three late-2025 attacks. Shai-Hulud 2.0 (November 2025) used a preinstall hook, installed the Bun runtime to dodge Node-focused monitoring, entered through a `pull_request_target` workflow, planted self-hosted runners, carried a destructive wiper and crossed into Maven Central through an automated mirror. GlassWorm (October 2025) hid executable code in invisible Unicode characters inside malicious VS Code and OpenVSX extensions, where human review cannot see it. The s1ngularity/Nx package invoked locally installed AI command-line tools (Claude, Gemini, Q) with permission-bypass flags and prompted them to hunt for wallets and credentials, turning developers' AI agents into the attack tool ([Xygeni](https://xygeni.io/?p=79254)).

### 2026 incident chronology

**[VENDOR]** StepSecurity's review of 22 August 2026, a vendor chronology and not a set of primary advisories, lists these 2026 incidents: on 19 to 24 March the "Team PCP" campaign poisoned Trivy Action (76 of 77 tags), Checkmarx KICS Action, telnyx and LiteLLM with a pipeline credential stealer; in March malicious axios releases 1.14.1 and 0.30.4 pulled a fake dependency that dropped a remote-access trojan, and the CanisterWorm npm worm appeared; in April and May "Mini Shai-Hulud" variants hit SAP-related, TanStack and AntV packages and intercom-client was hijacked; in June and July the "Miasma" worm planted workflows that exfiltrate secrets and reportedly targeted AI coding agents; and in August "ChainDrop" appeared on npm ([StepSecurity, 22 Aug 2026](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/state-of-open-source-supply-chain-attacks)). The page contains an internal inconsistency (72 against 73 affected Microsoft repositories), and the primary advisories were not opened.

### Scale indicators

**[VENDOR; relayed by a secondary source]** Sonatype is reported to have counted more than 454,600 new malicious open-source packages in 2025, up 75% year on year, with over 99% on npm, and further npm and PyPI waves in April and May 2026 are reported to have prompted an NHS England alert; a June 2026 Red Hat incident is also mentioned. These figures were relayed by a secondary source and not checked independently ([Patrowl, 29 June 2026](https://patrowl.io/en/blog/owasp-top-10-2025-what-s-changed-and-the-2026-data)).

### Recurring root causes and the lessons vendors draw

**[VENDOR]** StepSecurity (22 August 2026) names four recurring weaknesses: secrets present in pipelines and on developer machines, unpinned "latest" versions, open outbound network access from runners, and mutable GitHub Actions tags that a compromised maintainer can repoint. It argues that malicious code has usually already executed on a laptop or runner before production dependency scanning sees it ([StepSecurity, 22 Aug 2026](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/state-of-open-source-supply-chain-attacks)). Xygeni's lessons are to install strictly from lockfiles, minimise token scope, treat `pull_request_target` as high risk, audit self-hosted runners, scan source for unprintable Unicode, and filter egress on build and developer networks ([Xygeni](https://xygeni.io/?p=79254)).

### Minimum supply-chain control set

**[INFERENCE]** A minimum control set for a small SaaS team on GitHub and AWS follows from the incident lessons. Install from lockfiles only in the pipeline (`npm ci`, `pip --require-hashes` or `uv sync --locked`). Pin GitHub Actions to full commit SHAs and container images to digests. Disable or allowlist install scripts. Apply a cool-down before adopting freshly published versions (Renovate documents 14 days; see section 7), with security advisories exempt. Use OIDC federation from the pipeline to AWS and to registries, with no long-lived publish tokens. Generate a CycloneDX or SPDX SBOM per release and store it with the artifact. Sign container images and attach build provenance. Never use `pull_request_target` with a checkout of untrusted code. Put an egress allowlist on runners. Lockfile installs, OIDC, the `pull_request_target` rule and egress filtering are directly supported by the cited incident lessons; SHA pinning is supported by Renovate's guidance; the SBOM format choice is the researchers' judgement.

### SLSA build levels

**[FAST-MOVING, v1.2]** SLSA version 1.2 is the approved specification and has a Build track and a reintroduced Source track, with provenance and Verification Summary Attestations as the recommended attestation formats ([SLSA v1.2](https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/)). Build L0 gives no guarantees. Build L1 means provenance exists: a consistent build process whose platform automatically generates provenance (who built it, how, and the top-level inputs) and distributes it to consumers, which prevents mistakes but is trivial to forge. Build L2 means a hosted build platform generates and signs the provenance and consumers validate its authenticity, which protects against tampering after the build. Build L3 means a hardened platform where runs cannot influence each other and the signing secret is unreachable from user-defined build steps, which protects against tampering during the build, including by insiders or with stolen credentials ([SLSA v1.2 build track](https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/build-track-basics)).

**[INFERENCE]** SLSA Build L2 is reachable with a hosted pipeline that signs provenance (for example GitHub-hosted runners with artifact attestations), and L3 needs an isolated, hardened builder; whether any specific pipeline offering meets L3 was not verified. **[GAP]** The Source track level definitions, Sigstore and cosign, npm provenance, PyPI attestations, OpenSSF Scorecard and CISA's SBOM minimum elements were not read.

### SBOM and component due diligence under the Cyber Resilience Act

**[FAST-MOVING, applies in full from 11 Dec 2027; checked on primary source]** For products in scope, the Cyber Resilience Act's Annex I Part II(1) requires manufacturers to document components, including a software bill of materials in a commonly used machine-readable format covering at least top-level dependencies, and Article 13(5) and (6) require due diligence when integrating third-party components including open source, plus a duty to report vulnerabilities found in a component to its maintainer and to remediate them ([Regulation (EU) 2024/2847](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)). Scope is covered in section 11.

### Agent-specific supply chain

**[VENDOR]** The vendor summary of OWASP's agentic risk ASI04 asks for signed components, verified provenance, an AI bill of materials and a software-composition policy applied before an agent loads a new component ([Cycode](https://cycode.com/blog/owasp-top-10-agentic-applications/)). **[PLATFORM CLAIM]** Anthropic states that it reviews directory connectors against listing criteria but does not security-audit or manage any MCP server, so a listed connector is not a vetted one ([Claude Code security docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/security)).

### Open-source licence compliance

**[DURABLE; checked on primary source]** The new Product Liability Directive does not apply to free and open-source software developed or supplied outside a commercial activity (Article 2(2)), so a company that ships such software commercially is the liable manufacturer ([Directive (EU) 2024/2853](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402853)). The Cyber Resilience Act creates a lighter open-source software steward role (Article 24: a documented cybersecurity policy, cooperation with authorities and limited reporting), while commercial integrators carry full manufacturer duties ([Regulation (EU) 2024/2847](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)). **[PLATFORM CLAIM]** GitHub warns that its coding agent can produce output matching public code and asks for human review before merge ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/responsible-use/copilot-coding-agent)).

**[INFERENCE; no primary licence source was read]** A licence policy for agents is to allowlist permissive licences (MIT, BSD, Apache-2.0, ISC), require human or legal approval for copyleft (GPL, AGPL, LGPL), source-available (SSPL, BUSL, Elastic) and unlicensed code, run a licence scanner in the pipeline, and enable the assistant vendor's public-code-match filter where one exists. AGPL matters specifically for SaaS because network use triggers source-offer duties. **[GAP]** No source was read on measured licence contamination in AI output or on the copyright status of AI-generated code in the EU or Sweden.

## 11. EU regulation binding a software maker: four instruments and three near-term dates

### Orientation and the dates that matter in late 2026

**[FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026; legal orientation, not professional advice]** Three dates dominate late 2026 for a software maker selling in the EU. Cyber Resilience Act reporting duties have applied since 11 September 2026, with the full regulation applying from 11 December 2027 ([Regulation (EU) 2024/2847](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)). The new Product Liability Directive applies to products placed on the market or put into service after 9 December 2026 ([Directive (EU) 2024/2853](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402853)). Data Act switching charges are prohibited from 12 January 2027 ([Regulation (EU) 2023/2854](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202302854)). All three dates were checked on the primary EUR-Lex texts. Everything in this section and the next is orientation for engineering agents; scope decisions belong to the Legal department or outside counsel.

### Cyber Resilience Act: application dates and legacy products

**[FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026; checked on primary source]** Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, the Cyber Resilience Act, entered into force on 10 December 2024; its notified-body chapter applies from 11 June 2026, Article 14 reporting from 11 September 2026 and everything else from 11 December 2027 (Article 71) ([EUR-Lex 2024/2847](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847); [Commission CRA page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/cyber-resilience-act)). Under Article 69, products placed on the market before 11 December 2027 fall under the full requirements only if substantially modified after that date, but Article 14 reporting applies to all in-scope products placed before 11 December 2027 ([EUR-Lex 2024/2847](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)). **[CONTESTED; secondary source]** One law-firm note phrases the legacy reporting scope as products on the market before 11 September 2026; the regulation text is the authority ([CMS](https://cms.law/en/cze/legal-updates/ready-respond-report-the-cyber-resilience-act-s-reporting-regime-is-here)).

### Cyber Resilience Act: secure-by-default properties (Annex I Part I)

**[FAST-MOVING, applies from 11 Dec 2027; checked on primary source]** For in-scope products the Cyber Resilience Act turns several secure-default properties into legal requirements under Annex I Part I: no known exploitable vulnerabilities at release, secure-by-default configuration, access control, encryption of data at rest and in transit, integrity protection, data minimisation, a minimal attack surface, security logging and secure data removal ([Regulation (EU) 2024/2847](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)).

### Cyber Resilience Act: vulnerability handling (Annex I Part II)

**[FAST-MOVING, applies from 11 Dec 2027; checked on primary source]** Annex I Part II of the Cyber Resilience Act requires manufacturers of in-scope products to keep an SBOM, remediate vulnerabilities without delay, ship security updates separately from feature updates where feasible, test and review product security effectively and regularly, publish advisories for fixed vulnerabilities, run a coordinated vulnerability disclosure policy with a contact address, and distribute updates securely and free of charge ([Regulation (EU) 2024/2847](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)).

### Cyber Resilience Act: manufacturer duties and retention (Article 13)

**[FAST-MOVING, applies from 11 Dec 2027; checked on primary source]** Article 13 of the Cyber Resilience Act requires a documented cybersecurity risk assessment kept current through the support period, due diligence on third-party components, a support period matching expected use and at least 5 years unless the product's expected life is shorter, each security update kept available for at least 10 years or the rest of the support period, and technical documentation and the EU declaration of conformity kept for 10 years or the support period, whichever is longer ([EUR-Lex 2024/2847](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)).

### Cyber Resilience Act: the reporting clock (Article 14), in force now

**[FAST-MOVING, applies since 11 Sep 2026; checked on primary source]** Article 14 of the Cyber Resilience Act requires an early warning within 24 hours of becoming aware of an actively exploited vulnerability or a severe incident, a fuller notification within 72 hours, and a final report 14 days after a fix or mitigation is available for an exploited vulnerability or one month after the 72-hour notification for an incident ([EUR-Lex 2024/2847](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)). **[FAST-MOVING, 2026; secondary source only]** Reports go through ENISA's Single Reporting Platform to the coordinating CSIRT and ENISA, and a law firm noted the platform was not yet accessible when it wrote ([CMS](https://cms.law/en/cze/legal-updates/ready-respond-report-the-cyber-resilience-act-s-reporting-regime-is-here)). **[GAP]** The platform's operational status in November 2026 and Sweden's designated authorities for the Cyber Resilience Act were not verified.

### Cyber Resilience Act: penalties (Article 64)

**[FAST-MOVING; checked on primary source]** Article 64 of the Cyber Resilience Act sets fines of up to EUR 15 million or 2.5% of worldwide turnover for breaches of Annex I, Article 13 or Article 14, EUR 10 million or 2% for other listed duties, and EUR 5 million or 1% for misleading information; micro and small enterprises cannot be fined for missing the 24-hour early-warning deadline ([EUR-Lex 2024/2847](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)).

### Cyber Resilience Act: the SaaS boundary in the regulation text

**[FAST-MOVING; checked on primary source]** Under Article 3(2) of the Cyber Resilience Act, remote data processing is in scope only when the manufacturer designed it or it sits under the manufacturer's responsibility and the product could not perform one of its functions without it. Recital 12 says websites that do not support a product's functionality and cloud services outside the manufacturer's responsibility are out of scope, and that NIS2 is the instrument for cloud models such as SaaS, PaaS and IaaS. Article 3(30) defines a substantial modification as a post-market change that affects compliance with Annex I Part I or changes the assessed intended purpose ([EUR-Lex 2024/2847](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)).

### Cyber Resilience Act: the Commission's July 2026 guidance

**[FAST-MOVING, Jul 2026; secondary source only]** The Commission adopted application guidance for the Cyber Resilience Act on 27 July 2026 as Communication C(2026) 5252, with an annex built around 67 examples; it is not legally binding ([cyberresilienceact.eu, 27 Jul 2026](https://www.cyberresilienceact.eu/news/commission-cra-application-guidance-published-27-july-2026.html); [Osborne Clarke, 23 Sep 2026](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/european-commission-publishes-new-guidelines-cyber-resilience-act)). The Commission's own page confirms that practical guidance and an implementation FAQ exist ([Commission CRA page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/cyber-resilience-act)). For cloud and remote processing the guidance applies a three-part test: the processing happens remotely, the product could not perform one of its functions without it, and the software was designed under the manufacturer's responsibility ([Osborne Clarke, 23 Sep 2026](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/european-commission-publishes-new-guidelines-cyber-resilience-act)).

**[FAST-MOVING, Sep 2026; secondary source only]** A law-firm reading of the July 2026 guidance adds that a modification is substantial when it changes cybersecurity risk in a way the original risk assessment did not foresee or changes the intended use, after which the product counts as placed on the market again; that the support period is at least five years where expected use reaches that long and shorter or longer otherwise; and that open-source software outside commercial activity is excluded ([Osborne Clarke, 23 Sep 2026](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/european-commission-publishes-new-guidelines-cyber-resilience-act)).

### Cyber Resilience Act: does it reach a SaaS company?

**[INFERENCE; legal orientation only]** The scoping test for a SaaS company is to list every artefact a customer installs or runs: desktop or mobile app, browser extension, command-line tool, SDK or library, on-premises agent or connector, container image, or device firmware. Each one supplied commercially in the EU is likely a product with digital elements, and the back end it depends on is remote data processing inside that product's scope. Software offered purely as a hosted service, with nothing placed on the market as a product, is probably outside the Cyber Resilience Act and governed by NIS2 instead, but the researchers could not confirm that from the Commission's own text; treat it as probable and not settled. **[GAP]** The Commission annex with the SaaS, website and remote-processing examples was returned as an unreadable binary and must be opened before a scope decision ([Commission annex](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/131456)).

**[INFERENCE]** Because Article 14 reporting already applies to in-scope products including those already on the market, a company with any in-scope product needs four things today: an on-call path that can file within 24 hours, registration for the reporting platform, a decision record for exploited vulnerabilities, and a coordinated vulnerability disclosure policy with a public contact such as a security.txt file.

### Cyber Resilience Act: supporting instruments and preparation lists

**[FAST-MOVING, 2026; secondary source only]** Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2392 of 28 November 2025 describes the important and critical product categories, the first harmonised standards were expected in the third quarter of 2026, and vertical drafts exist for browsers, password managers, antivirus, VPNs, network management, SIEM and boot managers ([Hogan Lovells](https://hlc.com/en/publications/eu-cyber-resilience-act-getting-ready-for-cra-compliance-in-2026)). A law firm's action list (11 September 2026) is to inventory products and decide the company's role, build the 24-hour and 72-hour reporting procedure, run risk assessments, set support periods, prepare technical documentation and an SBOM, choose a conformity route and review supplier contracts ([Goodwin, 11 Sep 2026](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/09/alerts-lifesciences-technology-preparing-for-eu-cyber-resilience-act)).

### Product Liability Directive: software, including SaaS, is a product

**[FAST-MOVING, applies after 9 Dec 2026; checked on primary source]** Directive (EU) 2024/2853 treats software as a product (Article 4(1)); recital 13 says this holds however software is supplied, including through the cloud or a SaaS model, and that developers, including AI system providers, are manufacturers, while bare information and mere source code are not products. The directive applies to products placed on the market or put into service after 9 December 2026, and Member States must transpose it by that date (Articles 2(1) and 22) ([EUR-Lex 2024/2853](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402853)).

### Product Liability Directive: defect factors, updates and compensable damage

**[FAST-MOVING, applies after 9 Dec 2026; checked on primary source]** Under Directive (EU) 2024/2853, the factors for defectiveness include safety-relevant cybersecurity requirements and the effect of a product's ability to keep learning (Article 7(2)). A manufacturer stays liable for defects caused by software updates, or by the lack of updates needed to maintain safety, while the product is within its control (Article 11(2)). Compensable damage is death or personal injury including medically recognised psychological harm, damage to non-professional property, and destruction or corruption of data not used for professional purposes (Article 6) ([EUR-Lex 2024/2853](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402853)).

### Product Liability Directive: disclosure, presumptions and time limits

**[FAST-MOVING, applies after 9 Dec 2026; checked on primary source]** Under Directive (EU) 2024/2853 courts can order disclosure of evidence (Article 9) and presume defect or causation in listed cases, including non-disclosure, breach of mandatory safety requirements and technical complexity (Article 10). Liability toward the injured person cannot be excluded by contract (Article 15). The limitation period is 3 years, the expiry period 10 years, and 25 years for latent injury (Articles 16 and 17) ([EUR-Lex 2024/2853](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402853)).

**[INFERENCE; legal orientation only]** Product-liability exposure for B2B SaaS is narrower than it first looks, because compensable damage is limited to natural persons' injury, non-professional property and non-professional data. The real exposure sits in consumer-facing features, health or safety-relevant software, and components embedded in customers' consumer products. Since liability to the injured person cannot be contracted away, the department should keep evidence (design records, test results, update history, agent audit logs) able to answer a disclosure order, and should treat a missing security update as something that can itself make the product defective.

### Data Act: SaaS is a data processing service, and switching is a contract and engineering duty

**[FAST-MOVING, applies since 12 Sep 2025; checked on primary source]** Regulation (EU) 2023/2854, the Data Act, expressly names SaaS as a delivery model of a data processing service alongside IaaS and PaaS (Article 2(8) and recital 81) and has applied generally since 12 September 2025 (Article 50) ([EUR-Lex 2023/2854](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202302854); [Commission Data Act page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/data-act)).

### Data Act: mandatory switching terms (Article 25)

**[FAST-MOVING, applies since 12 Sep 2025; checked on primary source]** Article 25(2) of the Data Act requires contracts to give the customer a right to switch provider or port to on-premises infrastructure, with a notice period of at most 2 months and the transition completed within 30 calendar days (the provider may invoke up to 7 months if that is technically unfeasible, and the customer may extend once). Contracts must also cover assistance, continuity and security during switching, support for the customer's exit strategy, an exhaustive list of portable data categories and digital assets, a data retrieval window of at least 30 days after the transition, guaranteed full erasure afterwards, and a statement of any switching charges ([EUR-Lex 2023/2854](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202302854)).

### Data Act: switching charges end on 12 January 2027

**[FAST-MOVING, deadline 12 Jan 2027; checked on primary source]** Article 29 of the Data Act allows reduced, cost-based switching charges from 11 January 2024 to 12 January 2027 and prohibits switching charges from 12 January 2027 ([EUR-Lex 2023/2854](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202302854)).

### Data Act: technical and information duties for SaaS

**[FAST-MOVING, applies since 12 Sep 2025; checked on primary source]** Under Article 30(2) to (5) of the Data Act, PaaS and SaaS providers must offer open interfaces free of charge to customers and destination providers with enough documentation to build against them, achieve compatibility with common specifications or harmonised standards within 12 months of their publication, and otherwise export all exportable data on request in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format; IaaS providers instead owe facilitation of functional equivalence (Article 30(1)). Providers must publish switching and porting procedures, methods, formats and known limits and keep an online register of data structures, formats and standards for exportable data (Article 26), and must publish the jurisdiction of their infrastructure and their measures against conflicting third-country government access to non-personal data (Article 28) ([EUR-Lex 2023/2854](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202302854)).

### Data Act: exemptions, penalties and model clauses

**[FAST-MOVING; checked on primary source]** Article 31 of the Data Act exempts mostly custom-built services not offered at broad commercial scale from some duties and exempts non-production test and evaluation versions from the switching chapter; penalties are set nationally (Article 40) ([EUR-Lex 2023/2854](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202302854)). The Commission has published a draft recommendation on non-binding standard contractual clauses for cloud contracts ([Commission Data Act page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/data-act)). **[GAP]** The Swedish supervisory authority and penalty rules for the Data Act were not researched.

**[INFERENCE]** The Data Act engineering backlog for a SaaS vendor is a documented export API covering all customer-generated data and digital assets in an open format, a public register of data structures and formats, contract clauses matching Article 25 (with Legal), removal of exit and egress fees by 12 January 2027, and a published statement of infrastructure jurisdiction, which for AWS means the region or regions and the access posture of a US parent.

### GDPR: when a data protection impact assessment is needed

**[DURABLE; checked on the Swedish authority's page]** The Swedish data protection authority IMY explains that a data protection impact assessment (konsekvensbedömning) is required under Article 35 only for processing likely to cause high risk to individuals. Its minimum content is a systematic description of the processing and its purposes, an assessment of necessity and proportionality, an assessment of risks to individuals' rights, and the planned mitigations. The controller consults the data protection officer, seeks data subjects' views where appropriate, and requests prior consultation with IMY if high risk remains after mitigation; business risk is outside the scope of the assessment ([IMY konsekvensbedömning](https://www.imy.se/verksamhet/dataskydd/det-har-galler-enligt-gdpr/konsekvensbedomning/)).

**[INFERENCE; legal orientation only]** An engineering department should open a data protection impact assessment ticket when a feature adds large-scale processing of sensitive data, systematic monitoring, profiling with significant effects, or new AI processing of personal data. Sending customer personal data or production data to an AI coding agent or model provider needs a processor agreement and a transfer assessment. **[GAP]** The EDPB and IMY criteria lists, the Article 25 privacy-by-design text, EDPB Guidelines 4/2019, GDPR breach-notification timing and the transfer status of US sub-processors were not read.

### Swedish accessibility act: lag (2023:254)

**[FAST-MOVING, in force 28 June 2025; checked on primary source]** Sweden implements the European Accessibility Act, Directive (EU) 2019/882, through lag (2023:254) om vissa produkters och tjänsters tillgänglighet, in force since 28 June 2025 ([SFS 2023:254 on lagen.nu](https://lagen.nu/2023:254)). ### Swedish accessibility act: which services and products are covered

**[FAST-MOVING; checked on primary source]** Under 4 § of lag (2023:254) the covered services are electronic communications services, services giving access to audiovisual media, parts of passenger transport services, consumer banking services, e-books and e-commerce services, and they are covered when intended for consumers. An e-commerce service (2 §) is one provided at a distance through websites and mobile services, electronically, at a consumer's individual request, with a view to concluding a consumer contract ([SFS 2023:254, 2 § and 4 §](https://lagen.nu/2023:254)). Covered products include consumer computers and operating systems, payment and self-service terminals and e-readers ([Riksdagen SFS 2023:254](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-2023254-om-vissa-produkters-och-tjansters_sfs-2023-254/)).

### Swedish accessibility act: exemptions, transition and sanctions

**[FAST-MOVING; checked on primary source]** Under lag (2023:254), services provided by microenterprises need not meet the requirements (10 §), where a microenterprise has fewer than ten employees and annual turnover or balance sheet total of at most EUR 2 million (2 §). A requirement does not apply if it would fundamentally alter the service or impose a disproportionate burden (7 §), unless the operator received external funding to improve accessibility. Service contracts concluded before 28 June 2025 may run until expiry but no later than 27 June 2030, and pre-recorded media and document formats published before entry into force, and unedited archive content, are excluded. The government designates the supervisory and market-surveillance authorities (28 § and 30 §), and the sanction fee ranges from SEK 10,000 to SEK 10,000,000 (39 §), with none in minor cases ([SFS 2023:254](https://lagen.nu/2023:254); [Riksdagen SFS 2023:254](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-2023254-om-vissa-produkters-och-tjansters_sfs-2023-254/)). **[GAP]** The designation sits in förordning (2023:255), which failed to load; search-result titles point to Post- och telestyrelsen for services, which is unconfirmed.

**[INFERENCE; legal confirmation advised]** A B2B-only SaaS with no consumer contracts falls outside lag (2023:254) on the statute's wording, because covered services must be intended for consumers and e-commerce means concluding a consumer contract. A SaaS company is still affected when it sells to consumers, or when its customers (web shops, banks, e-book or transport providers) must comply and pass WCAG-level requirements down by contract. Target WCAG 2.2 AA through EN 301 549 in any case, because public-sector and enterprise buyers ask for it; run automated accessibility checks such as axe-core in the pipeline and test manually with keyboard and screen reader before release. ### Swedish NIS2 law: Cybersäkerhetslag (2025:1506), who is covered

**[FAST-MOVING, in force 15 Jan 2026; checked on primary source]** Sweden's Cybersäkerhetslag (2025:1506) entered into force on 15 January 2026. Its general rule (1 kap. 4 §) covers operators at or above medium-sized-enterprise size in a sector listed in NIS2 Annex I or II, and some operators are covered regardless of size (1 kap. 5 §), for example sole providers of an essential service and trust service providers. Under 1 kap. 7 § it covers operators with their main establishment in Sweden that offer cloud computing services, data centre services, content delivery networks, managed services, managed security services, online marketplaces, search engines, social networking platforms, DNS or domain registration services ([SFS 2025:1506](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/sfs-2025-1506.html)).

### Swedish NIS2 law: duties and incident clock

**[FAST-MOVING, in force 15 Jan 2026; checked on primary source]** Under Cybersäkerhetslag (2025:1506) a covered operator must notify the designated authority that it is in scope as soon as possible and report changes within 14 days (2 kap. 2 §), and its management must undergo security training (2 kap. 4 §). For a significant incident it must give early notification within 24 hours of becoming aware (2 kap. 5 §), an incident report within 72 hours (24 hours for trust service providers) (2 kap. 6 §), and a final report within one month of the incident report (2 kap. 8 §) ([SFS 2025:1506, 2 kap.](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/sfs-2025-1506.html)). **[FAST-MOVING, 2026; secondary source only]** A law-firm commentary reads the implementing regulation as starting the clock when the incident is identified as significant and advises keeping timestamps for awareness, severity assessment and reasoning, and planning for out-of-hours reporting and a portal-down fallback ([Legal 500 Sweden](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/sweden/privacy/cybersäkerhetslagen-senaste-nytt-efter-sommaren)).

### Swedish NIS2 law: sanctions and supervisor

**[FAST-MOVING, in force 15 Jan 2026; checked on primary source]** The sanction ceiling under Cybersäkerhetslag (2025:1506), 4 kap. 10 §, is the higher of 2% of worldwide annual turnover or the SEK equivalent of EUR 10 million for essential private operators, the higher of 1.4% or EUR 7 million for important private operators, and SEK 10 million for public operators; a management ban is possible for essential operators (4 kap. 6 §) ([SFS 2025:1506, 4 kap.](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/sfs-2025-1506.html)). ### The evidence pack an agent-run department should produce on demand

**[INFERENCE]** An agent-run engineering department should be able to produce ten artefacts on demand: a lifecycle policy mapped to SSDF; a branch-protection or ruleset export proving human review on the default branch; pipeline logs for static analysis, composition analysis, secret scanning and tests per release; an SBOM per release; a vulnerability register with timestamps against the patch policy; the latest external penetration-test summary with remediation status; a change log linking each production deploy to an approved pull request and an approving human; agent session logs and permission configuration; incident-response and vulnerability-disclosure policies; and a sub-processor list that includes AI model providers.

### Segregation of duties when agents write the code

**[INFERENCE]** Auditors testing change-management controls ask who approved a change. If an AI agent both authors and approves, segregation of duties fails, so every production change keeps a named human approver. ## 14. AI coding agents: what is measured and how they fail

### The honest summary of productivity evidence

**[INFERENCE]** Self-reports about AI coding tools are strongly positive, controlled measurements are inconclusive, and delivery outcomes depend on the engineering discipline that existed before the tools arrived. The supporting figures (DORA 2025, METR, Stack Overflow, JetBrains, Octoverse, CircleCI, GitClear) are in sections 5 and 6 of this document with their dates and links.

### Failure mode: insecure code at an unchanged rate

**[VENDOR]** Veracode's Spring 2026 update (published 24 March 2026) tested 80 tasks (4 languages by 4 CWEs by 5) with no security prompting and its own static analyser as judge. The overall security pass rate was 55%: Python 62%, C# 58%, JavaScript 57% and Java 29%. SQL injection passed at 82% and weak cryptography at 86%, but cross-site scripting passed at only 15% and log injection at 13%. Syntax correctness rose from about 50% to 95% over two years while the security pass rate stayed flat, and GPT-5-series models with extended reasoning reached 70 to 72% in the October 2025 update ([Veracode, 24 Mar 2026](https://www.veracode.com/?p=28620)).

**[INFERENCE]** Because security pass rates are flat while syntax correctness is near-perfect, "it compiles and the tests pass" is a weaker signal than it used to be. Mandatory static analysis and security-focused review must sit between agent output and merge, with rules for CWE-80 (cross-site scripting) and CWE-117 (log injection) given blocking status and extra attention paid to output encoding, log sanitisation and Java code.

### Failure mode: hallucinated packages and slopsquatting

**[FAST-MOVING, 2024 study]** An academic study of 16 code-generating models and 576,000 samples found at least 5.2% of package references from commercial models and 21.7% from open-source models were hallucinated, with 205,474 unique non-existent package names observed; the paper dates from 2024 and models have changed, so treat the rates as figures from that era ([arXiv 2406.10279](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10279)). **[INFERENCE]** The control is that an agent may add a dependency only when it exists in the registry with meaningful age and download history, passes composition and licence checks, arrives through a lockfile change visible in the pull request, and is approved by a human, combined with the cool-down in section 7. **[GAP]** No 2026 replication on current frontier models was found.

### Failure mode: prompt injection cannot be fully prevented

**[DURABLE]** The UK National Cyber Security Centre (8 December 2025) explains that prompt injection differs from SQL injection because language models have no boundary between data and instructions, so it may never be fully mitigated, and the model should be treated as an inherently confusable deputy. Its guidance is to use deterministic non-LLM safeguards, to give a model processing a party's content no more privilege than that party, to mark external data clearly, not to rely on deny-lists, and to log inputs, outputs, tool and API calls while watching failed tool calls ([NCSC blog, 8 Dec 2025](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blog-post/prompt-injection-is-not-sql-injection)).

**[VENDOR]** Real-world abuse described in a vendor write-up includes malware that invoked developers' local AI command-line tools with bypass flags (s1ngularity/Nx), invisible-Unicode payloads in editor extensions (GlassWorm), and a state-linked actor that used Claude Code to orchestrate intrusions against about 30 organisations by splitting work into innocuous-looking tasks (GTG-1002, disrupted in September 2025) ([Xygeni](https://xygeni.io/?p=79254)). **[PLATFORM CLAIM]** GitHub's agent filters hidden characters in issues and comments for this reason ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/responsible-use/copilot-coding-agent)).

**[INFERENCE]** A coding agent should treat as untrusted input: issue and pull-request text from outsiders, code comments and READMEs in third-party repositories, dependency source, web pages, tool and MCP responses, pipeline logs and error messages. Under the NCSC rule, a session reading such content holds no privilege beyond what the content's author should have, which means no production credentials and no push rights to protected branches.

### Failure mode: licence contamination

**[PLATFORM CLAIM]** GitHub acknowledges that its coding agent may produce output matching public code and requires human review before merge ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/responsible-use/copilot-coding-agent)); the licence policy is in section 10.

## 15. AI coding agents: the controls that published guidance agrees on

### The convergent control set

**[INFERENCE]** GitHub, OpenSSF, the Five Eyes agencies, the NCSC, OWASP, Anthropic and OpenAI agree on a control set a department can adopt as policy: agents work on branches and open pull requests only; a human merges; network access is off or allow-listed; no production or pipeline secrets sit in the agent environment; each agent has its own identity and a short-lived token; every tool call is logged; and hard rules live in hooks, deny rules and branch protection, not in the instruction file. The individual sources follow in this section.

### Five Eyes guidance on agentic AI (1 May 2026)

**[FAST-MOVING, May 2026; secondary summary of public-body guidance]** CISA, the NSA and Australia's ASD ACSC, with the cyber authorities of Canada, New Zealand and the United Kingdom, published "Careful Adoption of Agentic Artificial Intelligence (AI) Services" on 1 May 2026; it was read through a law-firm summary and the official PDF was not opened ([A&O Shearman, 20 May 2026](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-data/cisa-and-asds-acsc-publish-joint-guidance-on-securing-agentic-ai-systems); [official PDF, not opened](https://www.cyber.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/careful_adoption_of_agentic_ai_services.pdf)). The risk categories it names are weaknesses inherited from the underlying model (prompt injection, data poisoning), a larger attack surface from tools, memory and integrations, complexity that causes cascading failures, privilege and identity risk from over-permissioned agents, and reduced accountability and visibility ([A&O Shearman, 20 May 2026](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-data/cisa-and-asds-acsc-publish-joint-guidance-on-securing-agentic-ai-systems)).

**[FAST-MOVING, May 2026; secondary summary of public-body guidance]** The Five Eyes guidance of 1 May 2026 recommends starting with low-risk, non-sensitive tasks and widening autonomy only as controls mature, least-privilege access by design, a distinct identity per agent with continuous authentication, testing and red-teaming through the lifecycle, continuous logging of agent behaviour and tool use, human oversight of high-impact or irreversible actions, and planning for unexpected behaviour while favouring reversibility and containment over efficiency ([A&O Shearman, 20 May 2026](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-data/cisa-and-asds-acsc-publish-joint-guidance-on-securing-agentic-ai-systems)).

### NIST's agent standards work

**[FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026]** NIST launched an AI Agent Standards Initiative on 17 February 2026 (page updated 9 November 2026) with a request for information on agent security, a draft concept paper on software and agent identity and authorisation, and sector listening sessions ([NIST CAISI](https://www.nist.gov/caisi/ai-agent-standards-initiative)). No finished NIST control set for coding agents was confirmed.

### GitHub's agentic security principles

**[VENDOR]** GitHub's agentic security principles (25 November 2025) target three threats: data exfiltration, unclear attribution, and prompt injection through issues or repository files. Its six rules are to show all context and strip hidden Unicode or HTML before the agent sees it, firewall the agent's network access, withhold pipeline secrets and other repositories by default and revoke the agent token at session end, allow no irreversible change without a human (the agent can only open pull requests and never push to the default branch), give the agent a separate identity for clear attribution, and accept instructions only from users with write access. A person reviews and merges agent pull requests, and the pipeline on those pull requests does not run until a person starts it ([GitHub Blog, 25 Nov 2025](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/github-copilot/how-githubs-agentic-security-principles-make-our-ai-agents-as-secure-as-possible/)).

**[PLATFORM CLAIM]** GitHub's documentation for the Copilot cloud agent says it pushes only to its own `copilot/` branch or the pull-request branch, that Actions workflows on its pull requests wait for approval by a user with write access, that it has no access to organisation or repository Actions secrets (only a dedicated `copilot` environment), that a firewall against exfiltration is on by default, and that its commits are signed and co-attributed with session logs ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/responsible-use/copilot-coding-agent)). The page was read in November 2026.

### Anthropic: instructions are advisory, harness rules are deterministic

**[VENDOR]** Anthropic's Claude Code best-practices page (read 11 November 2026) draws the central distinction: instructions the model reads (prompt, CLAUDE.md, skills) are non-deterministic and usually but not always followed, while rules the harness enforces (hooks, deny rules, managed settings, branch protection) apply every time. Anything that must always or never happen therefore belongs in a hook, deny rule or managed setting. Deny rules hold in every permission mode, including auto and bypass modes, and side-effecting workflows such as a release can be made invocable only by a human (`disable-model-invocation: true`) ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).

### Anthropic: verification and review practice

**[VENDOR]** Anthropic's best-practices page (read 11 November 2026) advises giving the agent a pass or fail check (a test, a build exit code, a linter, a screenshot comparison) and asking for evidence, meaning the command and its output, enforced with a Stop hook that returns exit code 2 until the check passes. Review should be done by a fresh subagent that did not see the conversation, and reviewers should be told to skip style, because fixing every finding adds needless abstraction ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).

### Claude Code permission modes and the working-directory boundary

**[PLATFORM CLAIM; FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026]** Per Claude Code's security documentation as read on 11 November 2026, interactive sessions now start in auto mode, where a separate classifier model reviews risky actions and explicit ask and deny rules still apply; manual mode starts read-only and asks before edits and commands; and organisations can disable auto mode. The working-directory boundary is only a prompt, so an approved shell command can still write anywhere the user can, which makes OS-level sandboxing the stronger control ([Claude Code security](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/security)). **[INFERENCE]** Auto-approval modes that rely on a classifier model are themselves non-deterministic, so they do not replace deny rules and branch protection for actions that must never happen.

### Claude Code sandbox: what it covers and its limits

**[PLATFORM CLAIM; FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026]** Claude Code's sandbox is off by default; when enabled it uses Seatbelt on macOS or bubblewrap on Linux and WSL2, restricts writes to the working directory, and routes network traffic through a proxy with an initially empty domain allowlist that refuses link-local addresses such as 169.254.169.254. Its limits are that it covers shell commands only and not file tools, WebFetch, MCP servers or hooks; that reads are broad by default, so `~/.ssh` and `~/.aws/credentials` stay readable unless `denyRead` is set; that `failIfUnavailable: true` and `allowUnsandboxedCommands: false` are needed to prevent silent fallback; and that broad `excludedCommands` such as `docker *` defeat it. Agent configuration paths (`.claude/settings*`, hooks, `.mcp.json`, `.git/hooks`, shell rc files) are write-protected inside the sandbox so the agent cannot grant itself permissions ([Claude Code sandboxing](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sandboxing); [Claude Code security](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/security)).

**[INFERENCE]** Enabling the Claude Code sandbox is not enough by default: add `denyRead` for home-directory credential stores, an explicit domain allowlist and the fail-closed flags, and run the whole agent in a container or virtual machine for stronger isolation, because MCP servers and file tools sit outside that sandbox.

### Claude Code cloud sessions and team practice

**[PLATFORM CLAIM; FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026]** Claude Code cloud sessions run one isolated virtual machine per session with network limited by default and configurable to off or an allow-list; GitHub credentials never enter the machine because a proxy attaches them; the Git proxy rejects branch deletions and non-branch pushes; repository branch protection still decides what can be updated, and a rule that the connected access can bypass does not stop the agent; and all operations are logged. The team practices listed are managed settings for organisation policy, approved permission configurations in version control, usage monitoring through OpenTelemetry, hooks to audit or block settings changes, and dev containers or virtual machines for extra isolation. A non-interactive `-p` run shows no workspace-trust prompt and skips project MCP approval, so pipeline use needs its own controls ([Claude Code security](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/security)).

### OpenAI Codex defaults

**[PLATFORM CLAIM; FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026]** OpenAI's Codex documentation as read on 11 November 2026 sets network access off by default; in version-controlled folders it uses a workspace-write sandbox with on-request approvals (edits and commands inside the workspace run automatically, anything outside it or needing network asks), and in folders without version control it is read-only. Inside a writable workspace `.git`, `.agents`, `.codex` and `.aws` are kept read-only recursively, `danger-full-access` removes both sandbox and approvals and is discouraged, enabling network or web search exposes the agent to prompt injection because web results are untrusted, and enterprise administrators can enforce settings through managed configuration ([Codex agent approvals and security](https://developers.openai.com/codex/agent-approvals-security)). An earlier read of the same documentation adds that approval is needed for MCP and app tools annotated as destructive, that cloud runs go offline in the agent phase after set-up, and that an auto-review agent can handle approvals and fails closed ([Codex approvals and security, earlier URL](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/agent-approvals-security)).

### Anthropic's guide for security leaders

**[VENDOR]** Anthropic's guide of 17 July 2026 poses four assessment questions: what untrusted content can reach the agent, what it can do and as whom, what the blast radius is, and what is observable. Its controls are least agency, keeping credentials worth stealing out of the agent's environment and injecting them through a proxy, per-action (verb-level) tool permissions such as draft but not send and read but not delete, human review for destructive or one-way actions, egress allowlisting (described as the strongest control against injection-driven exfiltration), an organisation-wide kill switch, not relying on prompt scaffolds as controls, and governing agents like staff with identity, least privilege, monitoring and fast insider-risk response ([Anthropic CISO guide, 17 July 2026](https://claude.com/blog/ciso-guide-to-agentic-ai)).

### AGENTS.md as the shared instruction-file convention

**[FAST-MOVING, Nov 2026]** AGENTS.md is an open Markdown convention described as a README for agents, stewarded by the Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation and used by more than 60,000 open-source projects. Typical content is a project overview, build and test commands, code style, testing, security considerations and commit or pull-request rules; nested files are allowed, the nearest file wins, and explicit user prompts override it. It is supported by Codex, Cursor, the Copilot coding agent, Gemini CLI, Jules, Devin, Windsurf, Zed, VS Code and others ([agents.md](https://agents.md/)).

### What belongs in an instruction file

**[VENDOR]** Anthropic's best-practices page (read 11 November 2026) says CLAUDE.md should hold only what cannot be found by reading the repository: exact test, typecheck and lint commands, misleading code areas, decisions not recorded in the repository, and anything corrected twice. The memory guide recommends staying under 200 lines, path-scoped rules go in `.claude/rules/`, and team procedures go in skills, which load on demand ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).

**[DURABLE]** OpenSSF's Security-Focused Guide for AI Code Assistant Instructions (1 August 2025) keeps the developer accountable and puts AI-written code through the same review, testing and static analysis as human code. Its suggested instruction content is parameterised queries and input validation, no secrets in code, standard authentication libraries, no personal data in logs, safe defaults, negative tests for security-critical functions, confirming that a suggested package actually exists, pinned versions, and container images pinned by digest and run as non-root ([OpenSSF guide, 1 Aug 2025](https://best.openssf.org/Security-Focused-Guide-for-AI-Code-Assistant-Instructions)).

**[DURABLE]** The OpenSSF guide (1 August 2025) carries four caveats: keep instructions short and specific, since pasting the whole sample set makes assistants add irrelevant defensive code; persona prompts such as asking the assistant to act as a security expert are not recommended because some experiments found them worse; asking the assistant to critique and then revise its output reduced weakness density in one study; and the recommendations are preliminary ([OpenSSF guide, 1 Aug 2025](https://best.openssf.org/Security-Focused-Guide-for-AI-Code-Assistant-Instructions)).

**[INFERENCE]** The department's instruction file (AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md) should hold the exact build, test and lint commands, the definition of done (tests and scans green), the forbidden-actions list from section 16, the dependency and licence policy, the secrets rule (never read `.env`, never print or commit secrets), the data rule (no production or personal data in prompts), how to ask for approval, and pointers to deeper documents instead of long prose. Keep it short, exclude generic security boilerplate, and treat it as guidance for behaviour, because injected content can override instruction text. Instruction files and MCP configurations are themselves attack surface, since a malicious pull request can change them, so they sit under CODEOWNERS with mandatory human review.

### MCP security rules

**[FAST-MOVING, spec revision 2025-06-18]** The Model Context Protocol security best practices (revision 2025-06-18, which cross-links the 2025-11-25 revision) forbid token passthrough, so servers must not accept tokens not issued for them. Proxy servers must implement per-client consent, exact redirect-URI matching and a single-use short-lived OAuth `state` value to stop confused-deputy attacks. Servers must verify every request, must not use sessions for authentication and must use non-guessable session IDs. For local servers, clients must show the exact full command and get explicit consent before a one-click install and should sandbox servers with minimal filesystem and network rights, preferring stdio transport or authenticated local HTTP. Clients must validate authorisation URLs (http and https only), must not open them through a shell, and should block private IP ranges during OAuth discovery ([MCP Security Best Practices](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/basic/security_best_practices)).

**[PLATFORM CLAIM]** A project's `.mcp.json` does not show every server a Claude Code session can load, because user-scope servers, connectors and plugins add more; organisations can restrict servers through managed MCP configuration ([Claude Code security docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/security)). **[GAP]** The 2025-11-25 specification revision, any 2026 revision, and write-ups of real MCP incidents such as tool poisoning were not read.

## 16. Guardrails for the department's AI agents

### The principle: gates are enforced by the platform, not by the prompt

**[INFERENCE]** Published practice agrees on a principle and not on one canonical list: destructive, irreversible or trust-boundary-crossing actions need a human, and the gate must be enforced by the platform (permissions, branch protection, missing credentials) and not by the agent's instructions. The sourced anchors are the NCSC's rule on deterministic safeguards and privilege ([NCSC blog, 8 Dec 2025](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blog-post/prompt-injection-is-not-sql-injection)), Anthropic's guidance on one-way actions, verb-level permissions and credential-free environments ([Anthropic CISO guide, 17 July 2026](https://claude.com/blog/ciso-guide-to-agentic-ai)), GitHub's pull-request-only rule ([GitHub Blog, 25 Nov 2025](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/github-copilot/how-githubs-agentic-security-principles-make-our-ai-agents-as-secure-as-possible/)) and the Five Eyes recommendation of human oversight for high-impact or irreversible actions, read through a secondary summary ([A&O Shearman, 20 May 2026](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-data/cisa-and-asds-acsc-publish-joint-guidance-on-securing-agentic-ai-systems)). The three lists below map that principle onto engineering actions; items without a direct source are the researchers' mapping.

### Why a human stays accountable in law

**[DURABLE; checked on primary source]** Two legal drivers keep a human accountable for what agents ship. Manufacturer duties and the 24-hour reporting obligation under the Cyber Resilience Act sit with the company ([EUR-Lex 2024/2847](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)), and liability under the Product Liability Directive cannot be contracted away and covers defects introduced by updates ([EUR-Lex 2024/2853](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402853)). **[FAST-MOVING, May 2026; secondary press report]** Researchers quoted in the press advise keeping architecture and security design in human hands and reviewing AI output as one would a junior developer's ([TechCrunch, 29 May 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/29/coders-are-refusing-to-work-without-ai-and-that-could-come-back-to-bite-them/)).

### What agents may do alone

**[INFERENCE]** Under the sources in section 15, worker agents may act alone on these tasks: reading the repository; creating branches; writing code and tests; running tests, linters and scanners inside a sandbox; opening pull requests; drafting migrations, release notes, ADRs, postmortems and advisories; proposing dependency changes with a justification; and reviewing another agent's pull request as an additional, non-approving reviewer. Where the team has adopted risk-tiered oversight, low-risk changes that pass strong automated checks can be merged automatically under a rule humans have defined in advance, which is the Radar Volume 35 Trial practice ([Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)); the definition of "low-risk" is a human decision recorded in the ruleset and never an agent's judgement in the moment.

| File a regulator report, publish a security advisory, or state a legal or compliance position as fact | These are legal acts of the company | Draft-only tools | Inferred |
| Report work as done without evidence (the command run and its output) | Unverified output forwarded to others is the "meat proxy" anti-pattern | Stop hook that blocks completion until the check passes | Sourced in principle: [Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices); [Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques) |

### Approval quality and inter-agent trust

**[INFERENCE]** Approval quality matters as much as approval existence. Following the vendor summary of OWASP's ASI09, the human must see the actual diff, command or plan and not the agent's summary ([Cycode](https://cycode.com/blog/owasp-top-10-agentic-applications/)), and low-risk actions should be batched through allowlists so prompts stay rare and meaningful, which avoids the approval theatre the Radar warns about ([Radar techniques, Vol 35](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)). For a manager agent directing worker agents, the manager's approval is not a human approval; inter-agent messages should be authenticated and treated as untrusted for privilege purposes (ASI07), and every agent should have an owner, an expiry and a tested kill switch (ASI10) ([Cycode](https://cycode.com/blog/owasp-top-10-agentic-applications/)).

### Start narrow and widen autonomy as controls mature

**[INFERENCE]** The Five Eyes guidance (secondary summary, 20 May 2026) recommends starting agents on low-risk, non-sensitive tasks and widening autonomy only as controls mature ([A&O Shearman, 20 May 2026](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-data/cisa-and-asds-acsc-publish-joint-guidance-on-securing-agentic-ai-systems)). For this department that means the "may do alone" list grows only after the enforcing controls in the two tables exist and have been tested, and it shrinks again when main-branch success rate or change fail rate worsens. **[GAP]** GitHub ruleset setting names and AWS enforcement patterns (permission boundaries, service control policies, manual approval actions) were not read, so the control names above are generic.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of the question. Knowing best practice turns out to be the easy half: the durable engineering canon (small batches, fast tests, expand and contract, tenant isolation in the database, alarm-gated rollout) has barely moved in a decade, and 2026 data mostly reconfirms it. What has moved is where the constraint sits. With agents starting more than 40% of pull requests on GitHub and main-branch success falling in CircleCI's telemetry, the scarce resources are verification capacity and trustworthy gates, so the department's real product is evidence: a mutation-tested suite, a signed artifact with an SBOM, a diff a named human actually read, a timestamped incident record. Those same artefacts are what the Cyber Resilience Act, the Product Liability Directive, the Cybersäkerhetslag and the revised SOC 2 criteria ask for, which means one evidence pipeline can serve delivery, security, customers and regulators at once.

Two cautions travel with that conclusion. The first is that most numbers a manager agent will be tempted to steer by come from vendors measuring their own platforms, and the only controlled productivity trials are inconclusive by their authors' own account, so the department should baseline its own five delivery metrics before trusting anyone's benchmark. The second is that the legal picture for a pure SaaS company is still unsettled in exactly the place that matters most (whether the Cyber Resilience Act reaches it at all, and what the Swedish product liability act will finally say), and several trust-framework details in this document rest on recollection. Until the gaps in section 18 are closed at primary source, agents should treat every scope question as a handoff to a human and every "unverified" label as a stop sign.
